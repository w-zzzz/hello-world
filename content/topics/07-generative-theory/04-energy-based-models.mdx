Energy-based models are one of the oldest and most general probabilistic
frames in machine learning. They are also experiencing a quiet revival:
diffusion models, JEPA, contrastive learning, and even modern preference
learning are best understood as energy-based methods in disguise. If you
internalize the EBM picture, much of the modern generative landscape
reorganizes itself around a single thread.

## The basic object

An EBM defines an unnormalized probability through an **energy function**
$E_\theta(x)$:

$$
p_\theta(x) = \frac{\exp(-E_\theta(x))}{Z(\theta)}, \qquad Z(\theta) = \int \exp(-E_\theta(x))\, dx
$$

Low energy = high probability. The energy can be *any* neural network.
This is the EBM's superpower — and its curse, because the partition
function $Z(\theta)$ involves an intractable integral over the data
space.

Compare to other generative families:

- **Autoregressive models** (GPT) decompose $p(x) = \prod p(x_i | x_{<i})$
  with each factor explicitly normalized. No $Z$ problem, but the
  factorization is restrictive.
- **VAEs** use a tractable lower bound (the ELBO).
- **Normalizing flows** require invertible transforms with tractable
  Jacobians.
- **GANs** sidestep likelihood entirely.
- **EBMs** keep the maximum modeling flexibility and pay for it with $Z$.

Every EBM training method is a way to optimize $E_\theta$ without
computing $Z$.

## Training option 1: contrastive divergence

The exact MLE gradient is:

$$
\nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x) = -\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x) + \mathbb{E}_{x' \sim p_\theta}[\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x')]
$$

The first term is easy. The second is an expectation under the model —
which we can't sample from exactly. **Contrastive divergence** (Hinton)
approximates it with a few steps of MCMC starting from data points. CD
trained the original Restricted Boltzmann Machines and Deep Belief Nets,
and is still the canonical EBM training algorithm even though it's
biased (the chains don't fully mix).

## Training option 2: score matching

A more elegant approach: don't fit $p_\theta$ directly, fit its **score**:

$$
s_\theta(x) = \nabla_x \log p_\theta(x) = -\nabla_x E_\theta(x)
$$

Crucially, the score doesn't depend on $Z$ — taking a gradient with
respect to $x$ kills any $x$-independent constant. Hyvärinen (2005)
showed that minimizing $\| s_\theta(x) - \nabla_x \log p_{\text{data}}(x)\|^2$
can be rewritten using integration by parts into a form that doesn't
require knowing the data score at all.

Vincent (2011)'s **denoising score matching** is even more practical: add
Gaussian noise $\sigma$ to the data, fit $s_\theta(x + \sigma \epsilon, \sigma) \approx -\epsilon / \sigma$.
This is just MSE on noise.

```python
import torch.nn.functional as F

def denoising_score_matching(score_model, x, sigma):
    eps = torch.randn_like(x)
    x_noisy = x + sigma * eps
    score_pred = score_model(x_noisy, sigma)
    target = -eps / sigma
    return F.mse_loss(score_pred, target)
```

If this code looks familiar, it should: it's a sibling of the DDPM
training loss. Diffusion models *are* score-based EBMs trained at
multiple noise levels. The whole denoising-diffusion paradigm sits inside
the EBM frame.

## Sampling: Langevin dynamics

Once you have a score, you can sample from the EBM with **Langevin MCMC**:

$$
x_{t+1} = x_t + \frac{\eta}{2} s_\theta(x_t) + \sqrt{\eta}\, z_t, \qquad z_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)
$$

A walker following the score ascent with Gaussian noise converges to the
EBM's distribution. Practical issues — slow mixing, poor coverage of
disconnected modes — motivated the multi-noise-level construction of
Song & Ermon, which is also why diffusion samplers walk from high to low
noise.

## Why LeCun keeps pushing EBMs

LeCun's Position Paper (2022) argues that the natural frame for general
AI is energy-based. Two reasons:

1. **Multi-modality.** The future is not unique — many continuations of a
   video are plausible. A normalized probabilistic model has to spread
   mass across all of them; an energy function can give *all of them*
   low energy without forcing a global comparison.
2. **Joint embedding.** JEPA (also covered in the previous topic) is
   literally an EBM: $E(x, y) = \| \text{enc}(x) - \text{enc}(y)\|^2$
   measures compatibility between an input and its predicted
   representation. The training loss minimizes this energy on matched
   pairs while preventing collapse with implicit or explicit
   contrastive terms.

<Callout variant="insight" title="EBMs are everywhere if you squint">
Contrastive learning (CLIP, SimCLR) minimizes an energy between matched
pairs while pushing up energy between mismatched ones. Diffusion is
multi-scale denoising score matching. JEPA predicts in a learned latent
space using an energy. Even DPO has an energy-based interpretation —
the implicit reward is essentially a log-density ratio. Recognizing the
common skeleton clarifies a lot of the modern generative literature.
</Callout>

## Where EBMs struggle

- **Sampling cost.** Langevin chains and other MCMC are much slower than
  feedforward generation. Diffusion mitigates this with multi-noise
  schedules + cleverly designed reverse-time SDEs, but compute per
  sample is still high.
- **Evaluation.** Without $Z$, you can't compare likelihoods between
  models. EBM benchmarks rely on sample-quality metrics (FID,
  perplexity-on-test under bridge models) rather than direct
  log-likelihoods.
- **Mode coverage and spurious modes.** Implicit generation can create
  high-density regions where there is no data. Careful training and
  architectural inductive biases matter.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Don't equate 'no normalizer' with 'no problem'">
Skipping $Z$ in training doesn't mean you've avoided it. Sampling still
requires implicit access to relative densities; evaluating quality
requires picking a proxy; sample diversity requires good mode coverage.
The EBM frame buys flexibility but inherits a bouquet of subtleties that
normalized models do not have.
</Callout>
