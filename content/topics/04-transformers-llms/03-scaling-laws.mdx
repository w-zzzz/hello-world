Scaling laws are the closest thing deep learning has to a *physical theory*.
You vary one knob — compute, data, parameters — and the test loss falls in
a remarkably clean power law over many orders of magnitude. This empirical
regularity is what made the GPT roadmap navigable: you didn't need to know
*how* a 100B-parameter model would behave to be confident it would beat the
10B one.

Until 2022, the field's mental model came from Kaplan et al.'s 2020 paper,
which suggested compute should be poured disproportionately into making
models bigger. Then DeepMind's Chinchilla quietly demonstrated that the
field had been training nearly every model wrong.

## The empirical shape

Test cross-entropy loss as a function of model size $N$ (parameters), dataset
size $D$ (tokens), and compute $C$ (FLOPs) is well fit by:

$$
L(N) \approx L_\infty + \left(\frac{N_c}{N}\right)^{\alpha_N},\quad
L(D) \approx L_\infty + \left(\frac{D_c}{D}\right)^{\alpha_D}
$$

with empirically determined exponents $\alpha_N \approx 0.34$ and
$\alpha_D \approx 0.28$. The constants $L_\infty$ are the irreducible
entropy floors of the data distribution. Treat the formula as a
*calibration curve*: given a small-scale fit, you can extrapolate the loss
of a much larger run.

The compute relationship ties them together: ignoring constants,

$$
C \approx 6 N D
$$

(forward + backward FLOPs per token times $D$ tokens). For a fixed compute
budget, you have a Pareto frontier: bigger model, fewer tokens, or smaller
model, more tokens.

## Kaplan vs Chinchilla

Kaplan et al. (2020) ran sweeps and concluded the optimal allocation was
roughly $N \propto C^{0.73}$, $D \propto C^{0.27}$ — scale parameters fast,
data slowly. The whole field absorbed this. GPT-3 (175B parameters, 300B
tokens) followed the prescription: $D/N \approx 1.7$.

Hoffmann et al. (2022) — the **Chinchilla** paper — re-ran the sweep more
carefully, sweeping $N$ and $D$ jointly rather than fitting them
independently. Their conclusion was startling:

$$
N^* \propto C^{0.50},\quad D^* \propto C^{0.50},\quad D^* \approx 20\, N^*
$$

In other words, *for every parameter, train on about 20 tokens*. The
correction was massive: a compute-optimal version of GPT-3-class compute
should be a 70B-parameter model trained on 1.4T tokens, not a 175B model
trained on 300B. Their proof: a 70B Chinchilla beat the 280B Gopher (4×
larger, same compute) on most benchmarks.

<Callout variant="insight" title="A sociology lesson">
Two careful empirical papers, fit to similar data, disagreed on something
that determined the shape of an entire industry's training runs for two
years. Read both. Notice how the difference comes down to whether you let
the learning-rate schedule fully decay (Chinchilla did, Kaplan did not). At
this scale, methodology *is* the result.
</Callout>

## Why post-Chinchilla models train *past* the optimum

If $D^* \approx 20N^*$ minimizes training loss per FLOP, why does Llama 3
train a 70B-parameter model on **15T** tokens — over 10× the compute-optimal
data? Because compute-optimal is the wrong objective if you care about
inference.

Training cost is paid once. Inference cost is paid every time a user types
into the model. A 70B model trained on 15T tokens reaches the same loss as a
much larger compute-optimal model — but is far cheaper to *serve*. This
"over-training" recipe is the reason modern open-weights models are
dramatically smaller than scaling-law-purist models would predict.

```python
# Compute-optimal vs inference-optimal allocation
C_train = 1e25  # FLOPs
# Compute-optimal (Chinchilla):
import math
N_opt = (C_train / 6 / 20) ** 0.5
D_opt = 20 * N_opt
# Inference-optimal: pick smaller N, larger D for same C_train
N_inf = N_opt / 3
D_inf = C_train / (6 * N_inf)
print(f"Chinchilla:    N={N_opt:.2e}, D={D_opt:.2e}, D/N={D_opt/N_opt:.0f}")
print(f"Over-trained:  N={N_inf:.2e}, D={D_inf:.2e}, D/N={D_inf/N_inf:.0f}")
```

This trade-off is one of the most important practical decisions in modern
LLM design and depends on your serving volume.

## What scaling laws do *not* tell you

- They predict **loss**, not capability. The loss falls smoothly; specific
  capabilities (arithmetic, code, reasoning) often appear with phase
  transitions.
- They assume your data is "internet-text-like." Domain-shifted data has
  its own (worse) constants.
- They assume the architecture is approximately Transformer-shaped. Mamba,
  MoE, and RWKV all fit power laws — but with different prefactors.
- They are silent on **alignment, safety, and refusal behavior**. Cross-entropy
  going down does not mean the model is more useful or honest.

The frontier of 2024–2026 scaling research is no longer "how do I get a
lower loss" — that was settled. It's "how do I scale **test-time compute**
(reasoning models) and **data quality** (synthetic data, filtering) when raw
text is running out?" Both of these break the original Kaplan/Chinchilla
formulation because they change what $N$ and $D$ mean.
