A pre-trained GPT predicts the next token of internet text. That's not what
you want. You want an assistant: something that takes an instruction and
produces a helpful, honest, well-formatted answer. The transformation from
the first thing into the second — sometimes called *post-training* — is what
makes a usable product.

The 2022 InstructGPT paper laid out the recipe that everyone still uses, in
some form: **(1) Supervised fine-tune on instruction-response demonstrations.
(2) Train a reward model on human preferences. (3) Optimize the policy
against the reward model.** This module covers the first stage and the
adjacent topic of *prompting*, which lets you steer behavior without any
fine-tuning at all.

## Why a base model isn't an assistant

A base GPT trained on internet text has seen plenty of question-answer
patterns — but also forum flame wars, marketing copy, listicles, and code.
At inference, the model picks the continuation that is *most likely* given
the prefix. Ask "What is the capital of France?" and you might get the right
answer, or a list of geography quiz questions, or a Wikipedia stub. The
distribution of continuations is the distribution of the internet, which is
not the distribution of helpful answers.

SFT shifts the conditional distribution. By fine-tuning on millions of
(instruction, ideal-response) pairs, you push the model toward the
"helpful-assistant" mode of behavior. The architecture and loss don't
change; only the data does.

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y) \sim \mathcal{D}_{\text{instruct}}} \sum_{t} \log p_\theta(y_t \mid y_{<t}, x)
$$

where the loss is computed *only on the assistant's response tokens* — the
instruction is conditioned on, not predicted.

```python
from datasets import load_dataset
from trl import SFTTrainer, SFTConfig
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM

ds = load_dataset("HuggingFaceH4/ultrachat_200k", split="train_sft")
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B")
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B")

trainer = SFTTrainer(
    model=model,
    tokenizer=tok,
    train_dataset=ds,
    args=SFTConfig(output_dir="sft-llama", max_seq_length=2048,
                   per_device_train_batch_size=4, num_train_epochs=1),
)
trainer.train()
```

The whole loop is conceptually identical to pretraining; the magic is in the
*data*.

## Few-shot, zero-shot, and chain-of-thought

Even before SFT, large models exhibit non-trivial in-context learning. Three
prompting regimes have become standard vocabulary:

- **Zero-shot**: just the instruction. *"Translate to French: 'good morning'"*
- **Few-shot**: a handful of demonstrations precede the query, teaching the format and style implicitly.
- **Chain-of-thought (CoT)**: the prompt instructs the model to *reason out loud* — *"Let's think step by step."*

CoT was the most surprising of these. Wei et al. (2022) showed that asking
math-word-problem questions with CoT produced enormous accuracy gains on
large-enough models — and *zero* gains on small ones. CoT was the first
clear capability *emergence* tied to scale, and the conceptual seed of
modern reasoning models.

<Callout variant="insight" title="CoT is test-time compute, smuggled in">
Each reasoning token consumes another forward pass. A model that thinks for
500 tokens before answering has done ~500× more compute than a model that
answers immediately. This is why CoT works — and why o1, o3, and DeepSeek-R1
are essentially CoT made native, with the reasoning trace produced
internally and at much greater length. Prompting was the prototype; the next
chapter is the production version.
</Callout>

## Chat templates, system prompts, and tool definitions

Modern assistant models are fine-tuned with specific structural conventions:

- **Roles**: every turn is tagged `system`, `user`, or `assistant`.
- **Special tokens**: `<|im_start|>`, `<|user|>`, `<|assistant|>`, etc., delimit segments. These vary per model.
- **Tool/function calling**: tools are defined in the system prompt or via a structured schema; the model emits structured calls the harness executes.

Always use the tokenizer's `apply_chat_template` rather than concatenating
strings yourself:

```python
messages = [
    {"role": "system", "content": "You are a concise math tutor."},
    {"role": "user", "content": "Differentiate x^3 sin(x)."},
]
input_ids = tok.apply_chat_template(messages, return_tensors="pt",
                                    add_generation_prompt=True)
```

Skip this and you silently degrade every response.

## Prompting as programming, soberly

Prompt engineering deserves both more and less respect than it gets. *More*,
because the difference between a careless prompt and a careful one is often
a 10–30 point benchmark gap. *Less*, because most "prompt-engineering"
folklore (magic phrases, threats, bribes) does not survive controlled
evaluation. The robust techniques are mundane:

1. State the task precisely. Define inputs and outputs.
2. Show 2–5 well-chosen examples.
3. Specify the format (JSON schema, bullet list, exact length).
4. Ask for reasoning *before* the answer when the task is non-trivial.
5. Verify on a held-out set, not on vibes.

This is the prelude to reasoning models, agent frameworks, and RLHF — all
of which try to internalize one or another of these recipes so the user
doesn't have to.
