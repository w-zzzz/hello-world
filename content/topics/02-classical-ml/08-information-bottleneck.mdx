The information bottleneck (IB) is the most influential information-theoretic
lens on representation learning of the last twenty-five years. It promised a
principled answer to "what makes a representation good?" and provoked one of
the most public methodological disputes in modern deep learning.

It is worth knowing both for what it gets right — IB-flavoured objectives
genuinely improve robustness — and for what it does not — strong claims
about how SGD itself performs IB compression have not held up. Either way,
you will see the equations again in $\beta$-VAEs, in disentanglement, in
RLHF's KL-to-reference penalty, and in modern self-supervised losses.

## The principle

Suppose $Y$ is the variable you ultimately care about and $X$ is your input.
A representation $T$ is a stochastic function of $X$. The IB objective says
the optimal $T$ minimizes:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{IB}} = I(X; T) - \beta\, I(T; Y)
$$

In words: keep as little information about $X$ as you can while still keeping
as much about $Y$ as possible. The Lagrangian parameter $\beta \ge 0$ slides
between trivial constant representations ($\beta = 0$) and copying $X$
faithfully ($\beta \to \infty$).

The achievable points in the plane of $(I(X; T), I(T; Y))$ form the
**information plane**, and the IB curve is its Pareto frontier. Tishby's
elegant claim: a good representation lives near this frontier; a bad one is
strictly dominated.

## A clean self-sufficiency claim

For the special case where $X, Y$ are jointly discrete and you can solve the
self-consistent equations, the IB optimum is a *minimal sufficient statistic*
of $X$ for $Y$ — sufficient (no information about $Y$ thrown away), minimal
(nothing extra about $X$ kept). This is a satisfying generalization of the
classical sufficiency idea from statistics.

For continuous high-dimensional variables, things get hard. Mutual information
is notoriously difficult to estimate from samples; for invertible
deterministic networks $I(X; T)$ is technically infinite. This is where the
field split.

## The deep-learning controversy

In 2017 Schwartz-Ziv & Tishby reported empirically that during SGD training,
hidden representations of an MLP first **fit** ($I(T; Y)$ rises) and then
**compress** ($I(X; T)$ falls), tracing the IB curve. This was electric: SGD
itself, they argued, was implementing IB compression as a generalization
mechanism.

In 2018 Saxe et al. presented careful counter-experiments. Switch the
activation from tanh to ReLU and the compression phase disappears. The
"compression" Schwartz-Ziv & Tishby measured was largely an artifact of
binning saturating activations. Whether deep networks naturally compress
during training is *not* a settled empirical fact.

The strong theoretical claim — IB explains generalization in deep nets — has
not survived. The weaker engineering claim — IB-style stochastic
representations behave better — absolutely has.

## Deep VIB: the practical version

Alemi, Fischer, Dillon, Murphy (2017) showed how to *optimize* an IB-flavoured
objective with standard deep-learning machinery. Replace $T$ with a Gaussian
encoder $T \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_\phi(X), \Sigma_\phi(X))$, bound the two MI
terms variationally:

$$
I(X; T) \le \mathbb{E}_{p(x)}\big[\mathrm{KL}(p(t \mid x) \,\|\, r(t))\big]
$$

$$
I(T; Y) \ge \mathbb{E}_{p(x, y)}\big[\log q(y \mid t)\big] + H(Y)
$$

with $r(t)$ a fixed marginal prior and $q(y \mid t)$ a variational decoder.
Plug into a stochastic neural net classifier with the reparameterization
trick — the result is structurally identical to a $\beta$-VAE with a
classification head:

```python
mu, logvar = encoder(x)
t = mu + torch.randn_like(mu) * (0.5 * logvar).exp()
loss = F.cross_entropy(classifier(t), y) + beta * 0.5 * (mu**2 + logvar.exp() - 1 - logvar).sum(-1).mean()
```

What you get is: cleaner uncertainty, better adversarial robustness,
disentangled latents (small $\beta$ makes representations more
input-faithful, large $\beta$ encourages compression).

## The same equations everywhere

Once you have the IB Lagrangian in your head, you start spotting it
everywhere:

- **$\beta$-VAE** (Higgins et al. 2017) is exactly the IB objective with
  reconstruction as the prediction term.
- **RLHF KL penalty** $\mathbb{E}[\log \pi_\theta - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}]$
  is a constraint on how much information the new policy can carry beyond
  the reference — a kind of policy bottleneck.
- **Self-supervised contrastive losses** (SimCLR, CLIP) maximize
  $I(T_1; T_2)$ between two views — the prediction half of an IB.
- **Diffusion** can be cast as a chain of bottlenecks, each step compressing
  while preserving denoising-relevant information.

<Callout variant="insight" title="The honest summary">
The IB principle gave us an enormously productive *language* for talking
about representations. The strongest claims — that SGD does IB compression,
that IB explains generalization — are unproven or refuted. The medium-strength
claims — that information-theoretically motivated stochastic bottlenecks
(VIB, $\beta$-VAE) yield more robust and transferable representations —
are well-supported and absolutely worth using.
</Callout>
