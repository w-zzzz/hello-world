Bayesian inference is the most general framework we have for learning from
data. Every other inference rule — maximum likelihood, regularized regression,
even most regularizers in deep learning — can be derived as a special case of
the Bayesian update or of an approximation to it.

The reason it is worth your time even in 2026, with deep learning everywhere:
calibration, uncertainty, small-data regimes, and most of the modern
post-training stack (preference modeling, online RL with KL constraints) are
fundamentally Bayesian in character. You cannot read a modern alignment paper
and follow what the KL terms are doing without knowing where they come from.

## The single rule

Given a parameter $\theta$, data $D$, and a prior $p(\theta)$:

$$
p(\theta \mid D) = \frac{p(D \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)}{p(D)}
$$

The posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior. The denominator
$p(D) = \int p(D \mid \theta) p(\theta)\, d\theta$ is the *evidence* — a
normalizing constant in $\theta$, but a model-comparison signal in itself.

That single equation contains:

- **Maximum likelihood** ($\hat\theta_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\max p(D \mid \theta)$) is the posterior mode under a uniform prior.
- **MAP estimation** ($\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max p(\theta \mid D)$) is the posterior mode under any prior.
- **Ridge / Lasso** are MAP under Gaussian / Laplace priors on weights.
- **Marginal likelihood** $p(D) = \int p(D, \theta)\, d\theta$ is a built-in
  Occam's razor — it down-weights parameter values that fit *too* flexibly.

## Conjugacy: the closed-form sweet spot

A prior is **conjugate** to a likelihood when the posterior stays in the same
family. The Beta–Binomial pair is the canonical example:

$$
\theta \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha, \beta), \quad k \mid \theta \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, \theta) \;\Rightarrow\; \theta \mid k \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha + k, \beta + n - k)
$$

Updating is just adding successes and failures to the hyperparameters. No
integration, no sampler, no tears. The Gamma–Poisson, Normal–Normal,
Dirichlet–Multinomial, and Inverse-Wishart–Gaussian pairs are the other big
ones you will encounter — together they cover an embarrassing fraction of
applied Bayesian work.

## When conjugacy fails: MCMC

Most interesting posteriors are not conjugate. The standard answer is **Markov
chain Monte Carlo**: build a chain whose stationary distribution is
$p(\theta \mid D)$, run it long enough, and treat the samples as draws from
the posterior.

Random-walk Metropolis is the simplest variant — propose, accept-or-reject by
the ratio $\pi(\theta')/\pi(\theta)$ — but it scales poorly: in $d$
dimensions, the optimal step size shrinks as $d^{-1/2}$ and chains mix slowly.
**Hamiltonian Monte Carlo** (HMC) and its self-tuning cousin **NUTS** treat
$-\log p(\theta \mid D)$ as a potential energy and use gradient-driven
leapfrog integration to make long, informed proposals. PyMC, NumPyro, and
Stan all default to NUTS for this reason.

```python
import numpyro, numpyro.distributions as dist
from numpyro.infer import MCMC, NUTS
import jax.numpy as jnp, jax.random as random

def model(y):
    mu = numpyro.sample("mu", dist.Normal(0, 10))
    sigma = numpyro.sample("sigma", dist.HalfNormal(5))
    numpyro.sample("obs", dist.Normal(mu, sigma), obs=y)

mcmc = MCMC(NUTS(model), num_warmup=500, num_samples=2000)
mcmc.run(random.PRNGKey(0), y=jnp.array([1.2, 0.8, 1.0, 1.4]))
mcmc.print_summary()  # posterior of mu, sigma with effective sample sizes
```

## Variational inference: trade exactness for speed

When MCMC is too slow — large datasets, high dimensions, deep nets —
**variational inference** (VI) replaces the posterior by a tractable family
$q_\phi(\theta)$ and minimizes $\mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, p)$ by maximizing the
ELBO:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(\theta)}[\log p(D \mid \theta)] - \mathrm{KL}(q_\phi(\theta) \,\|\, p(\theta))
$$

Recognize this objective? You should — it is the same ELBO that VAEs
optimize, and the KL-to-prior term has the same shape as the KL-to-reference
penalty in RLHF. Bayesian inference shows up *everywhere* in modern ML; it
just usually wears a costume.

## Bayesian deep learning, briefly

The fully Bayesian dream — a posterior over every weight in a 70B model — is
not practical. Instead the field uses cheap approximations:

- **Deep ensembles**: train $K$ networks with different seeds. Their
  disagreement on a test input is a workable epistemic-uncertainty estimate
  and almost always beats more elaborate Bayesian methods on benchmarks.
- **MC Dropout**: keep dropout active at inference and average forward passes.
  Equivalent to a variational Bernoulli posterior at zero retraining cost.
- **Last-layer Laplace**: linearize around a trained model and put a Gaussian
  posterior on the final layer only. Cheap, well-calibrated, becoming
  standard in safety-critical applications.
