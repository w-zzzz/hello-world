A Gaussian process is the non-parametric Bayesian's preferred object. Instead
of picking a model class (linear, polynomial, neural net) and putting a prior
on its parameters, you put a prior directly on functions — and let the data
decide how complicated the function ought to be.

It is one of the few corners of classical ML where you can write down the
predictive distribution in closed form, get calibrated uncertainty for free,
and prove things. It also turns out to be the right lens for understanding
infinitely wide neural networks, which is the bridge to NTK and the modern
theory of deep learning.

## A distribution over functions

A function $f$ is drawn from a **Gaussian process** $\mathcal{GP}(m, k)$ if for
*every* finite collection of inputs $X = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ the outputs are
jointly Gaussian:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} f(x_1) \\ \vdots \\ f(x_n) \end{pmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N}\big(m(X),\, K(X, X)\big)
$$

where $m: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$ is a mean function (often taken to be
zero) and $k: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$ is a positive
semi-definite kernel encoding similarity.

That is the entire definition. Everything else — fitting, prediction, kernel
choice — is consequences.

## The kernel is the model

The kernel encodes your assumptions: what does it mean for two inputs to be
"similar"? Smoothness, length-scale, periodicity, lineary, all live here.

| Kernel | Sample functions look like |
|---|---|
| RBF / Squared exponential | Infinitely smooth bumps |
| Matérn-3/2, 5/2 | Smooth-but-realistic; default for spatial data |
| Linear | Bayesian linear regression in disguise |
| Periodic | Repeating with a learnable period |
| Sum / product of kernels | Compositional structure |

Choosing the kernel is the equivalent of choosing the model class. There is no
universal best kernel; reading Rasmussen & Williams chapter 4 once is the
single highest-ROI thing you can do here.

## Closed-form regression

Suppose you observe noisy targets $y_i = f(x_i) + \varepsilon_i$ with
$\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$. The posterior over $f$ at any
new point $x_*$ is Gaussian:

$$
\mu_*(x_*) = k_*^\top (K + \sigma^2 I)^{-1} y
$$

$$
\sigma_*^2(x_*) = k(x_*, x_*) - k_*^\top (K + \sigma^2 I)^{-1} k_*
$$

where $k_* = (k(x_*, x_1), \dots, k(x_*, x_n))^\top$. Predictive mean *and*
predictive variance, both closed-form, both calibrated under the model.

```python
import gpytorch, torch

class ExactGP(gpytorch.models.ExactGP):
    def __init__(self, x, y, likelihood):
        super().__init__(x, y, likelihood)
        self.mean = gpytorch.means.ConstantMean()
        self.cov  = gpytorch.kernels.ScaleKernel(gpytorch.kernels.MaternKernel(nu=2.5))
    def forward(self, x):
        return gpytorch.distributions.MultivariateNormal(self.mean(x), self.cov(x))

likelihood = gpytorch.likelihoods.GaussianLikelihood()
model = ExactGP(x_train, y_train, likelihood)
# Optimize log-marginal-likelihood for hyperparameters (length-scale, output-scale, noise).
```

The hyperparameters of the kernel (length-scale, output-scale, noise) are
themselves fit by maximizing the **log marginal likelihood**
$\log p(y \mid X)$ — Type-II maximum likelihood, also called *empirical Bayes*.
Marginal likelihood automatically penalizes models that are too flexible,
so kernel hyperparameter selection has built-in regularization. This is one
of the most elegant model-selection stories in classical ML.

## The $O(n^3)$ wall, and how to climb it

Inverting the kernel matrix is $O(n^3)$ in time and $O(n^2)$ in memory.
Practical work-arounds, in increasing order of complexity:

- **Inducing-point methods (SVGP, FITC)**: replace $X$ with $m \ll n$
  pseudo-inputs, paying $O(nm^2)$ instead.
- **Structured kernels**: if inputs lie on a grid, use Kronecker / Toeplitz
  structure for $O(n \log n)$ multiplies.
- **Conjugate gradient + Lanczos** (GPyTorch): solve linear systems iteratively
  on GPU; scales to $n > 10^6$ with care.
- **Deep kernel learning**: feed inputs through a neural net first, then GP
  on the latent — useful when raw inputs are images or sequences.

## Why GPs still matter in 2026

Three places GPs are still the best tool:

1. **Bayesian optimization**: tuning expensive black-box functions
   (hyperparameters, materials, robot policies). Vizier, BoTorch, Optuna's
   GP backend — all GP-based. The exploration-exploitation trade-off needs
   calibrated uncertainty, which GPs deliver.
2. **Geospatial and time-series modelling** with small to medium $n$,
   where calibrated uncertainty matters more than raw accuracy.
3. **Theory**: the NNGP / NTK correspondence (Neal 1996, Lee et al. 2018,
   Jacot 2018) shows that infinitely wide neural nets converge to GPs at
   initialization and to a kernel-regression solution under gradient flow.
   This is the cleanest analytical handle anyone has on deep learning.
