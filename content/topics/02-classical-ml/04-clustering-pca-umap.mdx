When you're handed a million 768-dimensional embeddings, your first move is
to look at them. But you cannot look at $\mathbb{R}^{768}$. You need a
projection — and the projection you choose tells a story about your data
that may or may not be true.

Dimensionality reduction is half intuition pump, half necessary
preprocessing for downstream models. It's worth knowing both halves: when
the resulting plot is trustworthy, and when it's a beautiful lie.

## Why this matters

Every modern ML pipeline produces high-dimensional embeddings — token
representations, image features, gene expression profiles, user vectors.
You evaluate them by clustering, visualizing, and retrieving. PCA, t-SNE,
and UMAP are the workhorse tools, and each makes different tradeoffs
between local fidelity, global structure, and interpretability.

K-means and GMM, meanwhile, are the canonical clustering methods. You'll
use them to label embedding clusters, initialize codebooks for VQ-VAE,
build hierarchical retrieval structures, and seed a hundred other
algorithms.

## PCA: the linear baseline

Given a centered data matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, PCA finds the
orthogonal directions of maximal variance. These are the top eigenvectors
of the covariance matrix $\frac{1}{n} X^\top X$, equivalently the right
singular vectors of $X$:

$$
X = U \Sigma V^\top, \qquad X V_k \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k} \;\; \text{is the rank-$k$ projection.}
$$

PCA preserves global linear structure exactly — pairwise inner products in
the projection equal those in the original space, up to truncation error.
It's interpretable, fast, and a defensible default. Its weaknesses are
exactly its strengths: linear, axis-of-maximum-variance, no notion of
manifold curvature.

<Callout variant="info" title="When PCA quietly fails">
A 'Swiss roll' embedded in 3D has 2D intrinsic structure, but PCA on it
produces a useless flat blob — the two best linear directions don't align
with the manifold's geodesic coordinates. You need nonlinear methods to
recover the underlying chart.
</Callout>

## K-means clustering

Pick $K$, alternate two steps until convergence:

1. **Assign**: each point to its nearest centroid.
2. **Update**: each centroid to the mean of its assigned points.

This is coordinate descent on the within-cluster variance objective

$$
\min_{\{\mu_k\}, \{z_i\}} \sum_i \|x_i - \mu_{z_i}\|^2
$$

K-means is fast, parallelizable, and gives reasonable results when clusters
are roughly spherical with similar variance. It assumes Euclidean geometry
matters, equal cluster size and density, and you know $K$ — none of which
are guaranteed. The elbow plot of within-cluster variance vs. $K$ is the
classical heuristic for picking $K$, but it's notoriously inconclusive.

For more flexible clustering: **Gaussian mixture models** (each cluster has
its own mean and covariance, fit with EM), **DBSCAN** (density-based, finds
arbitrary shapes), **HDBSCAN** (hierarchical density), **spectral
clustering** (cluster the eigenvectors of a similarity graph's Laplacian).

## t-SNE: matching local neighborhoods

t-SNE maps high-dimensional points to 2D such that *probabilistic*
neighborhood relations are preserved. In high-D:

$$
p_{j|i} = \frac{\exp(-\|x_i - x_j\|^2 / 2\sigma_i^2)}{\sum_{k \ne i} \exp(-\|x_i - x_k\|^2 / 2\sigma_i^2)}
$$

with $\sigma_i$ chosen so the conditional has a target perplexity. In 2D,
use a Student-$t$ kernel (heavy tails — that's the "$t$"):

$$
q_{ij} = \frac{(1 + \|y_i - y_j\|^2)^{-1}}{\sum_{k \ne l} (1 + \|y_k - y_l\|^2)^{-1}}
$$

and minimize $D_{KL}(P \,\Vert\, Q)$ over the 2D coordinates $y_i$.

t-SNE plots are extraordinarily good at revealing *cluster structure*. They
are also extraordinarily good at *fooling you*: cluster sizes, inter-cluster
distances, and density differences in the plot don't reliably reflect those
in the data. Distill's [How to Use t-SNE Effectively](https://distill.pub/2016/misread-tsne/)
is required reading before you put one in a paper.

## UMAP: faster, more global

UMAP frames the same problem topologically: build a fuzzy simplicial set in
high-D, build one in low-D, minimize a cross-entropy between them. The
practical wins:

- **Speed and scaling** — UMAP runs on millions of points in minutes.
- **More global structure** — inter-cluster distances are not arbitrary
  (though still not metric).
- **Useful as a preprocessor** — you can feed UMAP embeddings into
  downstream models, unlike t-SNE which is purely visualization.

```python
import umap, numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits

X, y = load_digits(return_X_y=True)

reducer = umap.UMAP(
    n_neighbors=15,    # local vs global tradeoff
    min_dist=0.1,      # how tightly points pack in low-D
    n_components=2,
    metric="euclidean",
    random_state=42,
)
emb = reducer.fit_transform(X)
print(emb.shape)   # (1797, 2)
```

The two knobs that matter: `n_neighbors` (small → emphasize local
structure; large → emphasize global) and `min_dist` (small → tight
clusters; large → smoother layout).

<Callout variant="warning" title="Visualizations are not ground truth">
A clean t-SNE/UMAP plot suggesting clean clusters is *evidence*, not proof.
Always validate with a second method: cluster in the original space and
color the plot, run a stability analysis, or check whether the structure
persists under different hyperparameters.
</Callout>

## What to do next

Build a pipeline you'll reuse forever: PCA to 50 dimensions, then UMAP to
2D, then color by whatever metadata you have. This combination is faster,
more numerically stable, and often more readable than UMAP on raw
high-dimensional inputs. Then run K-means or HDBSCAN on the 50D PCA output
for cluster labels. That's the standard recipe for embedding analysis at
every modern ML lab.
