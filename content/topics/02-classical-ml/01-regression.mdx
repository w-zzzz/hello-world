Regression is where statistics meets ML. It's also where most students first
realize that "loss function" isn't a design choice you make in isolation —
it's a probabilistic assumption about how your data was generated. Pick the
wrong loss and you've quietly fit the wrong model.

This topic ties together least squares, logistic regression, and
cross-entropy under a single banner: **maximum likelihood estimation**.

## Why this matters

Every supervised learning problem you'll meet — from sklearn's
`LinearRegression` to a 70B-parameter LLM's next-token predictor — is some
form of regression with a loss derived from a likelihood. Once you see this,
the menu of "which loss should I use?" shrinks dramatically. You pick a
distribution that matches your output, and the loss writes itself.

## Linear regression as MLE

Assume the target is the model's prediction plus Gaussian noise:

$$
y = w^\top x + b + \varepsilon, \quad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)
$$

The log-likelihood of a dataset under this model is

$$
\log L(w, b) = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_i (y_i - w^\top x_i - b)^2 + \text{const}
$$

Maximizing log-likelihood is exactly minimizing **mean squared error**.
That's it — that's why MSE is the default for real-valued regression. It's
not because squares are mathematically convenient (though they are); it's
because Gaussian noise is the maximum-entropy distribution given a fixed
variance, and MSE is its log-likelihood.

The closed-form solution is the **normal equations**: $w^* = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y$.

## The bias–variance tradeoff

Regression error decomposes as

$$
\mathbb{E}[(y - \hat{f}(x))^2] = \underbrace{(\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}(x)] - f(x))^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\mathbb{V}[\hat{f}(x)]}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{noise}}
$$

Simple models have high bias, low variance. Complex models, the reverse.
Regularization deliberately raises bias to cut variance — and sometimes the
total error drops. This is the whole game in classical ML, and a surprising
amount of intuition for deep learning carries over (think: implicit
regularization from SGD, weight decay, dropout).

## Ridge and Lasso

Add an $\ell_2$ penalty:

$$
\hat{w} = \arg\min_w \|y - Xw\|_2^2 + \lambda \|w\|_2^2
$$

This is **ridge regression**. It has a closed form too — $w^* = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^\top y$ —
and the regularizer makes $X^\top X + \lambda I$ invertible even when $X^\top X$
is singular (collinear features).

Replace the $\ell_2$ with $\ell_1$ and you get **Lasso**. The $\ell_1$ ball
has corners on the coordinate axes, so the optimum often sits at zero for
many features — automatic feature selection. Ridge shrinks; Lasso shrinks
and selects.

<Callout variant="insight" title="Probabilistic view of regularization">
Ridge = Gaussian prior on weights. Lasso = Laplace prior. Maximum a
posteriori (MAP) estimation under those priors gives exactly these
penalties. "Regularization" and "Bayesian prior" are two names for the same
mathematical move.
</Callout>

## From regression to classification: logistic and softmax

For binary classification, model $p(y=1 \mid x) = \sigma(w^\top x + b)$ with
$\sigma$ the sigmoid. The Bernoulli log-likelihood is

$$
\log L = \sum_i y_i \log p_i + (1 - y_i) \log (1 - p_i)
$$

— **binary cross-entropy** with a minus sign. For $K$-way classification,
replace sigmoid with softmax and Bernoulli with categorical, and you get
plain cross-entropy. There is no "switch" between regression and
classification; you switched the noise model and the math followed.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

# regression
y_hat = torch.randn(32)
y     = torch.randn(32)
mse   = F.mse_loss(y_hat, y)              # Gaussian likelihood

# classification
logits = torch.randn(32, 10)
labels = torch.randint(0, 10, (32,))
ce     = F.cross_entropy(logits, labels)  # categorical likelihood
```

## Why cross-entropy beats MSE for classification

You *can* train a classifier with MSE on softmax outputs. It works badly.
The reason: MSE's gradient through softmax contains a saturation factor that
vanishes precisely when predictions are confidently wrong — exactly when you
most want a strong learning signal. Cross-entropy's gradient simplifies to
$(\hat{p} - y)$, which is large for confidently wrong predictions and small
for confidently right ones. This is the right shape.

<Callout variant="info" title="Picking a loss in five seconds">
- Real-valued, symmetric noise: MSE.
- Real-valued, heavy-tailed: MAE / Huber.
- Binary: BCE (with logits, not probabilities — for stability).
- Multi-class: cross-entropy.
- Counts: Poisson NLL.
- Ranking / preference: Bradley–Terry / pairwise logistic.
</Callout>
