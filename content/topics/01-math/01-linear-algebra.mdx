Linear algebra is the language the rest of machine learning is written in. Before
talking about gradients or attention or diffusion, we need to be fluent in the
nouns: **vectors, matrices, subspaces, eigenvalues**.

You don't need to memorize proofs. You need a working intuition for what each
operation does to data.

## Why this matters

Every operation in a neural network is a tensor operation. A linear layer is a
matrix multiply. Self-attention is three matrix multiplies plus a softmax. A
gradient update is a vector subtraction. Cosine similarity, PCA, contrastive
loss, low-rank adapters — all linear algebra.

When training breaks (exploding gradients, dead neurons, NaN losses) the cause
is almost always something that linear algebra would have warned you about:
ill-conditioned matrices, mismatched shapes, unstable inversions.

<Callout variant="insight" title="The whole field in one sentence">
Deep learning is a way to pick the parameters of a very large composition of
linear maps and pointwise nonlinearities so that, after enough data, the
function approximates something useful.
</Callout>

## The vocabulary you need

A **vector space** is a set on which you can add and scalar-multiply. The
vectors a network works with — token embeddings, image patches, hidden states —
all live in real-valued vector spaces $\mathbb{R}^d$.

A **linear map** $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ is one that respects
addition and scaling: $f(\alpha x + y) = \alpha f(x) + f(y)$. Every such map is
representable as a matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$ with $f(x) = Wx$.

The **rank** of $W$ is the dimension of its column space: the size of the
largest set of linearly independent columns. Rank tells you how much
information the map can preserve.

The **inner product** $\langle x, y \rangle = x^\top y$ measures alignment.
Almost every notion of "similarity" or "alignment" you see in deep learning
reduces to a normalized inner product.

## Singular Value Decomposition

Every real matrix factors as

$$
W = U \Sigma V^\top
$$

with $U, V$ orthogonal and $\Sigma$ diagonal with non-negative entries (the
singular values). SVD diagonalizes the action of $W$ in two steps:

1. Rotate the input by $V^\top$.
2. Stretch each axis by the corresponding singular value.
3. Rotate the output by $U$.

This is the picture behind PCA, low-rank approximation (the basis of LoRA), and
the spectral analysis you see in interpretability papers.

```python
import numpy as np

W = np.random.randn(64, 128)
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(W, full_matrices=False)

# Top-k low-rank reconstruction
k = 8
W_lowrank = (U[:, :k] * S[:k]) @ Vt[:k]
print("Reconstruction error:", np.linalg.norm(W - W_lowrank, ord="fro"))
```

If most of the singular value mass is in the top few components, the matrix is
well-approximated by a low-rank factorization — which is exactly the structural
assumption that makes LoRA fine-tuning so effective.

## Norms and conditioning

The **Frobenius norm** $\|W\|_F^2 = \sum_{ij} W_{ij}^2$ is the natural notion
of "size" for weight matrices. The **spectral norm** $\|W\|_2 = \sigma_{\max}$
is the largest singular value and bounds how much $W$ can stretch any input.

Spectral norm controls Lipschitz constants, which controls how badly small
input perturbations explode through the network. Spectral normalization (used
in some GAN variants) directly clips this.

The **condition number** $\kappa(W) = \sigma_{\max} / \sigma_{\min}$ measures
how badly $W$ stretches some directions relative to others. A high condition
number is the linear-algebraic signature of the gradient pathology that
momentum and Adam are designed to fix: the loss surface looks like a long,
narrow ravine with steep walls and a shallow floor. Optimizers that ignore
this structure either oscillate across the ravine or crawl along the floor.
Preconditioning (Shampoo, K-FAC) literally tries to invert the curvature so
that every direction is on the same scale.

<Callout variant="info" title="Three things to memorize">
1. Matrix–vector multiply: $O(mn)$.
2. The SVD always exists, and the top-$k$ truncation is the best rank-$k$ approximation in Frobenius norm.
3. A symmetric matrix has a full set of orthonormal eigenvectors; a covariance matrix is symmetric and PSD.
</Callout>

## Eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and what they tell you

For a square matrix $A$, the equation $A v = \lambda v$ identifies the
*invariant directions* of $A$: along $v$, the linear map only stretches by
$\lambda$. The set of $\lambda$ is the **spectrum**. For a symmetric (or
Hermitian) matrix, eigenvalues are real and the eigenvectors form an
orthonormal basis — the **spectral theorem**.

Two places this is load-bearing in deep learning:

- **Hessian eigenvalues** describe local curvature of the loss. Their
  distribution explains why some training runs are stable and others diverge.
  Sharpness-aware minimization, the "edge of stability" papers, and most
  optimizer theory are eigenvalue arguments at heart.
- **Covariance matrix eigenvalues** are PCA. The top eigenvectors of
  $X^\top X$ are the directions of maximum variance — the "natural" axes of
  the data. Whitening transforms align inputs to make optimization easier.

<Callout variant="insight" title="Linear maps as geometry, not symbols">
Every matrix you see in a paper has a geometric interpretation. $W_Q K^\top$
in attention is just *measuring angles*. A LayerNorm is a *projection onto a
sphere followed by a learned rescale*. A residual connection is the
*identity map plus a small perturbation*. If you find yourself confused by
notation, draw the picture.
</Callout>

## The matmul is the model

A useful sanity check: count the matmuls in any architecture and you've
counted essentially all the FLOPs. A Transformer block at hidden size $d$,
sequence length $T$, with MLP factor 4, costs roughly:

$$
\text{FLOPs/block} \approx \underbrace{8 T d^2}_{\text{Q,K,V,O proj}} + \underbrace{2 T^2 d}_{\text{attention scores+out}} + \underbrace{16 T d^2}_{\text{MLP}}
$$

For modest $T$ the MLP dominates; for long context attention takes over.
This single counting exercise is what makes scaling laws and tensor-
parallelism sharding decisions tractable. *Everything* about LLM systems —
why FlashAttention matters, why MoE saves compute, why TP splits along the
"head" axis — comes back to where this matmul work is.

## How to study this

You don't need to derive everything from scratch — you need an
*operational* understanding. Practical study path:

- Watch 3Blue1Brown's [Essence of Linear Algebra](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLZHQObOWTQDPD3MizzM2xVFitgF8hE_ab) (3 hours, total).
- Skim Strang's [MIT 18.06](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-06-linear-algebra-spring-2010/) lectures on SVD, eigenvectors, and orthogonality.
- Read the linear algebra appendix of Goodfellow et al.'s *Deep Learning* book.

Once these are second nature, every paper you read becomes legible: that
"$Q K^\top / \sqrt{d_k}$" in the attention formula stops being notation and
starts being geometry.
