If you only learn one piece of math beyond linear algebra for ML, learn the
basics of probability and information theory. Loss functions, generative
models, RL exploration bonuses, calibration, sampling — they are all
information theory in different costumes.

The remarkable thing is how few primitives you actually need. Three concepts —
**likelihood, entropy, and KL divergence** — generate most of the field.

## Why this matters

Cross-entropy loss is maximum likelihood. Variational autoencoders optimize a
KL-bounded ELBO. Diffusion models match score functions, which are gradients
of log densities. RLHF's reward modeling is a Bradley–Terry likelihood. Even
the temperature you set in `softmax` is a Lagrange multiplier on entropy.

When you read a new ML paper and see something that looks like a derivation
out of nowhere, nine times out of ten the author is just rewriting one of
these three quantities.

<Callout variant="insight" title="The unifying picture">
A model is a distribution $q_\theta$. A dataset is samples from a true
distribution $p$. Almost every ML objective is some form of "make $q_\theta$
close to $p$ in KL" — what differs is how you measure it and what tractable
upper bound you minimize.
</Callout>

## Random variables and expectation

A random variable $X$ takes values $x \in \mathcal{X}$ with probability $p(x)$.
The **expectation** is $\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}[f(x)] = \sum_x p(x) f(x)$
(or an integral in the continuous case). Almost every loss in ML is
$\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p_\text{data}}[\ell(x; \theta)]$, estimated by the
sample mean over a minibatch.

Two distributions worth memorizing:

- **Bernoulli$(p)$**: a single biased coin. Logistic regression's output.
- **Categorical$(\boldsymbol{\pi})$**: a $K$-sided die with probabilities $\pi_k$.
  The output of every classification head ever.

## Entropy: how surprising is a distribution?

The **Shannon entropy** of a discrete distribution $p$ is

$$
H(p) = -\sum_x p(x) \log p(x)
$$

It is the average information content (in nats if $\log$ is natural; bits if
log base 2) of a sample. Uniform distributions are maximally entropic;
delta functions have $H = 0$. Entropy bounds how well any code or model can
compress samples from $p$ — Shannon's source coding theorem.

In ML you'll see entropy as a *regularizer*: maximum-entropy RL adds
$\beta H(\pi)$ to keep policies stochastic; label smoothing softens targets
toward higher entropy to combat overconfidence.

## KL divergence: distance between distributions

$$
D_{KL}(p \,\Vert\, q) = \sum_x p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\!\left[\log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right]
$$

KL is non-negative ($\ge 0$, with equality iff $p = q$ almost everywhere) but
not symmetric and not a metric. The asymmetry is *the* point: $D_{KL}(p \Vert q)$
penalizes putting $q$-mass where there's no $p$-mass — "mode-covering". The
reverse, $D_{KL}(q \Vert p)$, penalizes putting $q$-mass where there's no
$p$-mass either, but in the opposite direction — "mode-seeking". VAEs
minimize one direction; some GAN variants the other; this single sign choice
explains a lot of qualitative differences in generative models.

## Cross-entropy = MLE

Cross-entropy is

$$
H(p, q) = -\sum_x p(x) \log q(x) = H(p) + D_{KL}(p \,\Vert\, q)
$$

If $p$ is the empirical distribution (one-hot on the true label), then
$H(p, q) = -\log q(y_\text{true})$, the **negative log-likelihood** of the
correct class. Minimizing cross-entropy on a dataset is *exactly* maximum
likelihood — they are the same algorithm written in different notation.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

logits = torch.randn(4, 10, requires_grad=True)   # (B, classes)
target = torch.tensor([3, 1, 4, 1])

# Cross-entropy = NLL on log-softmax. Same gradient, same number.
ce = F.cross_entropy(logits, target)
nll = F.nll_loss(F.log_softmax(logits, dim=-1), target)
print(torch.allclose(ce, nll))   # True
```

## Likelihood, in one sentence

Given data $\{x_i\}$ assumed iid from $p_\theta$, the **likelihood** is
$L(\theta) = \prod_i p_\theta(x_i)$ and the **log-likelihood** is
$\sum_i \log p_\theta(x_i)$. Maximum likelihood estimation picks the $\theta$
that makes the data most probable. Most modern objectives are either
exact MLE (autoregressive LMs), a tractable lower bound on MLE (VAEs' ELBO),
or a KL-equivalent surrogate (score matching, contrastive divergence).

<Callout variant="info" title="Three formulas to keep in your pocket">
1. Cross-entropy decomposition: $H(p, q) = H(p) + D_{KL}(p \Vert q)$.
2. Mutual information: $I(X; Y) = D_{KL}(p_{XY} \Vert p_X p_Y)$ — how far joint is from independent.
3. ELBO: $\log p(x) \ge \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x, z)] - \mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z)]$.
</Callout>

## What to do next

Read MacKay's *Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms* —
chapters 2, 4, and 22 in particular. It is free, beautifully written, and
ends up being the prerequisite for variational inference, MDL,
compression-based generalization arguments, and a half-dozen modern papers.

Once these three quantities feel automatic, ELBO derivations stop looking
like magic and start looking like bookkeeping.
