Two of the most counterintuitive empirical phenomena in modern deep
learning sit in the same place: a model behaves badly, then suddenly,
without obvious cause, behaves well. Double descent and grokking are
both transitions across regimes that classical statistics did not
predict and most ML pedagogy still under-explains.

They matter both because they are real engineering phenomena and
because they expose the gap between bias-variance intuition and what
modern over-parameterized models actually do.

## Why this matters

If you believe the bias-variance U curve, you set model capacity to
match the dataset and avoid overfitting. Modern practice does the
opposite: train models with billions of parameters on millions of
examples, drive training loss to zero, and ship. This works. The
classical theory does not explain why. Double descent is the empirical
correction; grokking is its cousin in time.

## Double descent: the big picture

Belkin et al. (2018) and Nakkiran et al. (2019) plotted test error
against model size *and* training time. The curve is not a U. It is a
**double descent**:

1. **Under-parameterized regime** (capacity ≪ data): test error
   decreases as capacity grows. Classical bias-variance, as expected.
2. **Interpolation threshold** (capacity ≈ data): test error spikes.
   The model can just barely fit the training set, and the unique
   interpolating solution is wildly variable.
3. **Over-parameterized regime** (capacity ≫ data): test error
   *decreases again*, often below the underfit minimum.

The same shape appears as a function of training time (epoch-wise
double descent) and effective model size (sample-wise double descent).

<Callout variant="insight" title="Implicit bias is the explanation">
Past the interpolation threshold there are *many* zero-loss solutions.
SGD doesn't pick one at random; it has an implicit bias toward
low-norm, flat-minimum solutions. Those solutions tend to generalize.
The benign-overfitting story is: SGD + over-parameterization +
implicit regularization = a quiet win that classical theory missed.
</Callout>

## Why this is not a paradox

Classical theory bounds generalization via VC dimension or Rademacher
complexity — capacity measures that grow with the number of
parameters. Modern theory replaces these with capacity measures that
depend on the *solution found*: norms, margins, NTK eigenstructure.
The model class is huge; the implicitly-selected solution is small.

Double descent is the empirical fingerprint of this transition. At
the interpolation threshold, the implicit bias has nothing left to
work with — the unique fitting solution is whatever it is — so test
error blows up.

Plot $h$ vs error and the U-then-down shape becomes visible whenever
$N \approx d \cdot h$ — the parameter count crosses the data count.

## Grokking: late-onset generalization

Power et al. (2022) trained a small transformer on **modular addition**
modulo a prime, with weight decay, on a few thousand examples. The
training accuracy hit 100% within a few thousand steps. Test accuracy
stayed at chance.

Then, around 100,000 steps later — long after anyone watching a loss
curve would have stopped — test accuracy *abruptly* rose to ~100%.
The network had been *memorizing*, then started *generalizing*.

## What is happening during the wait?

Nanda et al. (2023) reverse-engineered a grokking modular-addition
transformer and found a remarkable result: the network learns a
**discrete Fourier algorithm**. Inputs are mapped to sinusoidal
embeddings; the output is computed via trig identities. Mechanistically,
the algorithm is fully explicit.

What happens before grokking, then? The network is *gradually
constructing* the Fourier algorithm in parallel with the memorization
circuit. Weight decay disadvantages the high-norm memorizer relative to
the structured generalizer. At some point the generalizing circuit is
sharp enough to take over, the memorizing circuit is decayed enough to
yield, and accuracy on novel inputs flips on.

This is the deepest current explanation for grokking: **competition
between two solutions, with weight decay as the tiebreaker**, plus the
mechanistic picture that the slow buildup is the construction of an
algorithm.

## Are these the same phenomenon?

They are siblings. Both are sharp transitions in test performance
without sharp transitions in training loss. Both depend critically on
implicit bias, weight decay, and over-parameterization. Both are
benign — the model ends up better than naive theory would predict.

The unifying view: the loss landscape contains both *memorizing*
(high-norm, fitting) and *generalizing* (lower-norm, structured)
basins. Training dynamics mediate which one you end up in. Sometimes
that takes a long time and looks like a phase transition.

<Callout variant="warning" title="What this does and does not justify">
Double descent does not mean "always train bigger." It means "do not
sit on the interpolation threshold." Grokking does not mean "train
forever." It means "with the right regularization and enough patience,
generalizing solutions can emerge from memorizing ones." Both are
phenomena to recognize and exploit, not heuristics to deploy blindly.
</Callout>
