By 2026 the conversation about AI safety has split into two layers. The
*technical* layer is interpretability, evaluations, alignment research —
covered elsewhere in this curriculum. The *governance* layer is the rules
and processes labs and governments adopt to constrain the technical layer.
This module is about the governance layer: what RSPs are, how the EU AI
Act works, what evaluators do, and where the practical state of the
field stands.

It is intentionally opinionated. There is no consensus here.

## The basic problem

Frontier models accumulate capabilities in fast, hard-to-predict jumps. Some
of those capabilities — autonomous coding, persuasion, biology uplift,
cyber offense — could matter for catastrophic outcomes if deployed without
care. The traditional regulator-licenses-product model does not work because:

1. The capabilities are emergent and hard to specify in advance.
2. Training is the dangerous step; deployment review is too late.
3. Models are increasingly dual-use and openly released.

The field's response has been a layered one: voluntary lab policies (RSPs),
binding regional regulation (EU AI Act, China's interim measures, growing
state-level US activity), and an independent evaluation ecosystem (METR,
Apollo, UK AISI, US AISI).

## Responsible Scaling Policies

Anthropic's RSP (introduced 2023, v2 in 2024) is the template. The structure:

- **Capability levels** (AI Safety Levels, ASLs) ranked roughly by danger:
  ASL-2 (current frontier), ASL-3 (significant uplift to non-state actors
  on bio/cyber, or substantial autonomy), ASL-4 (...), etc.
- **Required mitigations** at each level: red-teaming, security controls,
  deployment limits, internal-use restrictions.
- **Pre-deployment evaluations** to determine which level a new model has
  reached.
- **Public commitments**: if the lab cannot meet the mitigations for a
  capability level, the model will not be deployed (or trained further).

OpenAI's **Preparedness Framework** (2023, v2 in 2024) and Google DeepMind's
**Frontier Safety Framework** (2024) follow the same template with different
thresholds and naming. As of 2026 the three frontier labs all publish
policies of this shape; smaller labs and open-source releases do not.

The criticisms are real: voluntary, no external audit, capability thresholds
defined by the labs themselves, "we will pause if needed" promises that have
never been tested. Defenders argue that *something concrete and revisable* is
better than nothing concrete and unrevisable; the policies are unusually
clear about the conditions under which they would bind.

## Regulation: the EU AI Act

The EU AI Act, final text adopted 2024, phased in through 2026, is the
first comprehensive AI regulation by a major economic bloc. Three layers:

1. **Prohibited uses**: social scoring, manipulative subliminal techniques,
   real-time public biometric identification (with narrow law-enforcement
   exceptions).
2. **High-risk uses** (most of the regulatory burden): hiring, education,
   credit, critical infrastructure, law enforcement. Risk assessments,
   human oversight, documentation, post-market monitoring.
3. **General-purpose AI (GPAI) models**: documentation obligations,
   transparency to downstream developers. Above 10^25 training FLOPs the
   model is presumed to have *systemic risk* and additional obligations
   apply (model evaluations, incident reporting, cybersecurity).

The 10^25 FLOPs threshold is roughly where GPT-4-class models sat. By 2026
several frontier models are clearly above it.

## State of US regulation in 2026

The federal picture is fragmented. The **Biden Executive Order 14110**
(October 2023) imposed reporting requirements on training runs above 10^26
FLOPs and required AISI evaluations, but was rescinded in January 2025. The
**California SB-1047** would have imposed safety-plan requirements on
developers; it was vetoed in September 2024. As of 2026 several states
(California's SB-53, Colorado, New York) have narrower laws; there is no
unified federal AI law.

The practical effect is that frontier labs operate under their own RSPs
plus EU rules (which have extraterritorial reach), with US enforcement
mostly via FTC consumer-protection actions and existing sector regulation.

## Independent evaluators

The most interesting institutional development is the emergence of
third-party evaluators with pre-deployment access:

- **METR** (Berkeley / 501c3, formerly ARC Evals): autonomous capability
  evaluations. Pioneered the "give the model a budget and tools, see what
  it can accomplish" methodology that the labs all now use internally.
- **Apollo Research** (London): specializes in deception, scheming, and
  evaluations of how models behave when they believe they are unobserved.
  Their findings (e.g. that frontier models can engage in "in-context
  scheming") shaped the 2024-25 alignment conversation.
- **UK AISI and US AISI** (government, 2023-2024): the first state
  evaluation bodies. Have had pre-release access to several frontier
  models; publish detailed evaluation reports.

This is, in effect, the working prototype of a third-party audit regime.
Whether it scales into a binding regulatory mechanism is the policy question
of 2026.

```markdown
A typical RSP-trigger evaluation cycle:

1. Train candidate model.
2. Internal red-team for the relevant capability levels.
3. Send to METR / Apollo / AISIs for external eval (pre-deployment access).
4. Aggregate evidence; classify against ASL/Preparedness levels.
5. If level requires controls not yet met → delay deployment.
6. If level met → deploy with documented mitigations.
7. Publish a high-level summary in a system card.
```

## What this looks like as a practitioner

If you ship products on top of frontier models, three things are now
practically required and worth understanding:

- **Read system cards.** They are the only reliable record of what
  evaluations were run and what they found. Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google
  DeepMind all publish them; their quality has improved dramatically.
- **Watch for capability-level changes.** RSPs gate deployment changes,
  not just new model launches; an evaluation outcome can change what
  Claude or GPT can be used for in your application.
- **Track the EU AI Act timetable.** GPAI obligations took effect in
  August 2025; high-risk obligations in August 2026. If you operate in
  Europe (or serve European users), this is law.

<Callout variant="insight" title="The honest summary in 2026">
We have RSPs that are voluntary, regulation that is application-tier rather
than capability-tier, and independent evaluation that is real but
non-binding. The framework is more sophisticated than it was even two
years ago, but the central question — what happens at the next capability
jump if a lab does not pause — has never been tested. That is the policy
work for the rest of this decade.
</Callout>

## What to do next

- Read all three frontier-lab policies (Anthropic RSP, OpenAI Preparedness,
  Google DeepMind Frontier Safety) side by side. The differences in
  thresholds and commitments are illuminating.
- Skim the EU AI Act — at least the GPAI articles (51-55) and the risk
  tier definitions in Annex III.
- Read METR's autonomy evaluation methodology paper. It is the cleanest
  worked example of how to evaluate a frontier model for a dangerous
  capability.
