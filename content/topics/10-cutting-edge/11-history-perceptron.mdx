Modern deep learning likes to forget that this is the *third* time neural
networks have been the future of AI. The first time was Rosenblatt's
perceptron in 1958, and it ended badly. Knowing why is the cheapest
inoculation against repeating the mistakes.

This module is short. It is here because anyone who claims AI history
started with AlexNet (or with ChatGPT) is missing seventy years of context
that explains why the field works the way it does — including the
institutional shape of modern AI labs, the funding cycles, and the
defensive caution of researchers who lived through earlier crashes.

## 1958: the perceptron

Frank Rosenblatt was a Cornell psychologist building a physical neural
network. The Mark I Perceptron, completed in 1960, was a 400-photocell
camera connected via potentiometers (the weights, adjusted by motors) to
hard-threshold output units. Given a picture, it learned to discriminate
classes by the perceptron update rule:

$$
w \leftarrow w + \eta\, (y - \hat{y})\, x
$$

Novikoff (1962) proved this converges in finite steps for any linearly
separable dataset. That convergence proof is one of the first formal
learning guarantees in machine learning.

The press coverage was sensational. The *New York Times* in 1958 reported
that the perceptron would soon "walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself,
and be conscious of its existence." Rosenblatt himself was somewhat more
restrained, but the field's reputation for grand claims was being seeded.

## 1969: Minsky and Papert

Marvin Minsky (MIT) and Seymour Papert (MIT) published *Perceptrons: An
Introduction to Computational Geometry* in 1969. The book is a serious
piece of mathematics. Its key results:

1. A single-layer perceptron cannot represent XOR (linearly inseparable).
2. Certain *global* geometric predicates (parity, connectedness) cannot
   be computed by a single-layer perceptron whose receptive fields are
   bounded — even with infinite weights and infinite training time.
3. Multi-layer networks could in principle compute these things, but no
   efficient training algorithm existed.

The technical content was correct. The rhetorical effect was much larger
than the technical scope. Funding agencies and the broader research
community read the book as "neural networks don't work." Symbolic AI
absorbed most of the field's resources for the next fifteen years.

In hindsight, the missing ingredient was an efficient way to train
multi-layer networks. Werbos (1974), then Rumelhart, Hinton, Williams
(1986), would supply it with backpropagation. By then the first winter
was at its deepest.

## 1973: the Lighthill Report

In 1972 the UK Science Research Council commissioned applied mathematician
James Lighthill to evaluate the state of AI research. His 1973 report
concluded that:

- The field had failed to deliver on its grand promises.
- The "combinatorial explosion" made search-based methods scale poorly to
  realistic problems.
- Most general AI funding should be cut; resources should go to applied
  subfields (machine vision, speech) where progress was concrete.

The BBC televised a debate in 1973 — Lighthill versus John McCarthy, Donald
Michie, and Richard Gregory — that you can still watch on YouTube. UK AI
funding was sharply reduced afterward. DARPA in the US also pulled back.
The combination of Lighthill in the UK and the post-Mansfield-Amendment
DARPA cuts in the US produced what historians now call the first AI winter
(roughly 1974-1980).

## What actually went wrong

Three structural failures, all of which have modern echoes:

1. **Overclaiming**. The perceptron's press coverage — "perceptron will be
   conscious" — set expectations that no reasonable progress could meet.
2. **Generalizing from a specific architecture's limits to all of AI**.
   Minsky-Papert's correct mathematical result about single-layer
   perceptrons was read as a refutation of neural networks in general.
3. **Funder fatigue**. Five-year promises plus five years of progress that
   does not match those promises produces five-year retreats.

The *next* AI winter (1987-1993, the LISP-machine and expert-systems
collapse) followed essentially the same template — different technology,
same dynamics.

## What this episode bequeathed to us

- **Backpropagation took 28 years to be rediscovered for ML**. Werbos
  (1974) had it; the connectionist community did not absorb it until
  Rumelhart-Hinton-Williams (1986). A whole generation of progress was
  delayed by funding and attention.
- **A culture of skepticism about hype**. The neural-network researchers
  who survived (Hinton, LeCun, Bengio) are unusually careful about
  capability claims; the field's older voices remember 1969 vividly.
- **A political case for pluralism in AI research**. Putting all funding
  on one paradigm makes the next winter worse. The 2020s healthy
  diversity of approaches (transformers + SSMs + diffusion + neuro-symbolic)
  is, in part, a hedge.

```text
Rough chronology to remember:
  1943   McCulloch-Pitts neuron — formal model
  1958   Rosenblatt's perceptron, press explosion
  1962   Novikoff convergence theorem
  1969   Minsky-Papert Perceptrons
  1973   Lighthill Report → first AI winter
  1974   Werbos's PhD: backprop for nets
  1986   Rumelhart-Hinton-Williams: backprop popularized
  1989   LeCun: convolutional nets on MNIST
  1997   LSTM (Hochreiter-Schmidhuber)
  2006   Hinton: deep belief nets — neural nets re-emerge
  2012   AlexNet — the second renaissance begins
```

<Callout variant="insight" title="Why this is in a 2026 ML curriculum">
Every modern AI researcher will live through at least one capability
plateau and one funding compression. The first winter is the cleanest
case study of how *correct mathematics* combined with *overclaimed
capability* can produce a decade-long retreat. The lessons — be precise
about what your architecture cannot do, do not let the press extrapolate
without correction, and value researchers who survived the previous
winter — are still operational.
</Callout>

- Watch the 1973 BBC Lighthill debate (it's on YouTube). It is the most
