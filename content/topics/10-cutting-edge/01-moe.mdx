The single most important architecture change in frontier LLMs since the
Transformer is sparsity. Frontier models are no longer dense matmuls all
the way down. They are sparse: a giant pool of "expert" sub-networks, of
which only a tiny fraction runs for any given token.

Mixtral 8x7B (2024) was the first widely-used open-weight MoE. DeepSeek-V3
(671B total, 37B active) is the current existence proof that you can train
trillion-class models compute-efficiently. Both are bets on the same idea:
**parameters are cheap; compute per token is the budget**.

## Why this matters

Scaling a dense Transformer means every token pays for every parameter. If
parameters could be selectively activated based on the input, you could
keep adding capacity without adding per-token FLOPs. That is exactly what
MoE does. Done well, it gives you a model that knows more without taking
longer to evaluate.

This is no longer fringe. By 2025 a substantial fraction of frontier
production models — Mixtral, DeepSeek-V2/V3, Qwen-Moe, GPT-4-class systems
(by widely-reported architecture leaks), Gemini variants — are MoE.

## The architecture

Replace the MLP block of a Transformer layer with $E$ parallel MLPs (the
experts). A small **router** $g(\cdot)$ — usually a single linear layer
followed by softmax — produces routing weights for each token, and only
the top-$k$ experts are actually evaluated:

$$
y = \sum_{i \in \text{top}_k(g(x))} g(x)_i \cdot \mathrm{Expert}_i(x)
$$

For Switch Transformer, $k = 1$. For Mixtral and DeepSeek-V3, $k = 2$ and
$k = 8$ respectively. With $E = 8, k = 2$ you have 4× more parameters per
MoE layer at constant per-token compute.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

class TopKMoE(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim, hidden, num_experts=8, k=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.k = k
        self.gate = nn.Linear(dim, num_experts, bias=False)
        self.experts = nn.ModuleList([
            nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(dim, hidden), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(hidden, dim))
            for _ in range(num_experts)
        ])

    def forward(self, x):  # x: (B, T, D)
        logits = self.gate(x)                       # (B, T, E)
        weights, idx = logits.topk(self.k, dim=-1)
        weights = F.softmax(weights, dim=-1)
        out = torch.zeros_like(x)
        for slot in range(self.k):
            for e, expert in enumerate(self.experts):
                mask = (idx[..., slot] == e)
                if mask.any():
                    out[mask] += weights[..., slot:slot+1][mask] * expert(x[mask])
        return out
```

(Production implementations replace those Python loops with batched
matmuls, expert-parallel all-to-all, and grouped GEMM kernels — see
Megablocks, ScatterMoE, and the kernels inside vLLM.)

## The two hard problems

### 1. Routing collapse

Routers are positive-feedback systems: an expert that gets more tokens
trains faster and gets even better. Without intervention, a handful of
experts dominate and the rest are dead weight. Switch Transformer
introduced an **auxiliary load-balance loss**:

$$
\mathcal{L}_\text{aux} = E \cdot \sum_{i=1}^{E} f_i \, P_i
$$

where $f_i$ is the fraction of tokens dispatched to expert $i$ and $P_i$
is the average router probability for $i$. Both quantities equal $1/E$
under uniform routing, so the loss is minimized there. Add this with a
small coefficient (usually 0.01) and experts diversify.

DeepSeek-V3 takes a more elegant route: drop the loss entirely, and
instead carry a **per-expert bias** that the optimizer adjusts online to
keep loads balanced. The training objective stays purely linguistic; the
bias absorbs the balancing.

### 2. Token routing across devices

In multi-device training, experts are split across GPUs (expert
parallelism). Tokens must be **all-to-all**'d to their assigned experts,
processed, and **all-to-all**'d back. Imbalanced load means GPUs idle
waiting for the busy ones. **Capacity factors**, expert-choice routing
(experts pick top-$k$ tokens, dual to tokens picking top-$k$ experts), and
the new generation of fused dispatchers (DeepEP) all aim at this.

<Callout variant="insight" title="Inverting the routing direction">
Token-choice routing can drop tokens when an expert hits capacity.
Expert-choice (Zhou et al., 2022) flips the relation: each expert picks
its top-$k$ tokens. This guarantees perfect balance and removes dropped
tokens, at the cost of decoder-side complications (a token's route now
depends on its peers).
</Callout>

## Why MoE training is compute-efficient

Empirically, an MoE with $N$ total parameters and $A$ active per token
behaves *roughly* like a dense model with parameters somewhere between
$A$ and $\sqrt{NA}$ — far better than $A$, far worse than $N$, but
fantastic per active FLOP. The exact scaling depends on routing quality,
expert specialization, and data.

The DeepSeek-V3 report shows the practical upshot: 671B parameters
trained at the cost of a roughly 37B-active dense model, achieving
frontier benchmarks at a fraction of the compute budget of comparable
dense systems.

## Inference: not free

The catch is that MoE moves the bottleneck from compute to memory.
Inference at small batches loads all expert weights but uses very few of
them — terrible arithmetic intensity. This is why MoE serving systems
push hard on:

- **Expert parallelism**: experts split across GPUs so the entire model
  sits in aggregate HBM.
- **Larger batches**: more tokens per step means each expert sees enough
  work to saturate.
- **Quantization**: more important than for dense models because memory,
  not FLOPs, is the binding constraint.

## Open questions

MoE has won at training, but several research questions are open:

- How to do MoE in the attention layer (MoA, switch-head). Current
  approaches still use dense attention.
- How many experts is "enough"? DeepSeek-V3 uses 256; the right number
  with respect to data and compute is unsettled.
- Routing as an explicit modeling primitive — DeepMind, Anthropic, and
  others are exploring routers conditioned on context, not just on
  the current token.

<Callout variant="aside" title="Sparsity is older than you think">
The 2017 Shazeer et al. paper introduced sparse-gating to LSTMs. The
Transformer paper followed months later. MoE's mainstream rise had to
wait for distributed system primitives — all-to-all kernels, expert
parallelism, balanced routing — to mature, which took roughly five years.
</Callout>
