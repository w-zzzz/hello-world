The frontier of LLM training has split into two worlds. The first
trains $1{-}10$ trillion-parameter models on raw web data. The second
takes the outputs of the first — soft labels, generations, preferences —
and uses them to make small, fast, sharp models. Distillation, model
merging, and synthetic-data pipelines are how the second world works.

## Why this matters

Frontier models are too expensive to deploy widely. Distillation is the
mechanism by which their capability propagates downward — into the
on-device assistants, the edge models, the cheap-to-run domain
specialists. Most products you use are running a distilled or merged
descendant of a much larger thing.

## Knowledge distillation: the old idea, freshly relevant

Hinton, Vinyals, Dean (2015) framed distillation as: train a small
**student** model to match a large **teacher**'s output distribution
rather than just the hard labels.

$$
\mathcal{L}_\text{KD} = T^2 \cdot \mathrm{KL}\!\left( \mathrm{softmax}(z_t / T) \, \| \, \mathrm{softmax}(z_s / T) \right) + \alpha \cdot \mathrm{CE}(y, z_s)
$$

The temperature $T$ softens the teacher's distribution. At $T=1$ you
mostly get the hard label. At $T=4$ or so, the relative probabilities
across non-target classes become visible — the **dark knowledge** that a
teacher knows "5 looks like 6 more than like 1."

For LLMs, the modern equivalents include:

- **Sequence-level distillation**: the student is trained on the
  teacher's *generations*, not just its logits.
- **Logit distillation** at every token, where memory permits.
- **Reasoning distillation**: train the student on chain-of-thought
  traces produced by the teacher (the entire R1-Distill family).

The R1-Distill series (DeepSeek, 2024) is a striking demonstration: take
a strong reasoning teacher's CoTs, fine-tune a smaller base on them, get
a substantial fraction of the reasoning capability at a fraction of the
cost.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def kd_loss(student_logits, teacher_logits, labels, T=4.0, alpha=0.5):
    soft = F.kl_div(
        F.log_softmax(student_logits / T, dim=-1),
        F.softmax(teacher_logits / T, dim=-1),
        reduction="batchmean",
    ) * (T * T)
    hard = F.cross_entropy(student_logits, labels)
    return alpha * soft + (1 - alpha) * hard
```

## Model merging: arithmetic on weights

A more recent surprise: you can do arithmetic on the *weights* of fine-
tuned models and end up with something useful. Define the **task vector**
of a fine-tune as

$$
\tau = \theta_\text{ft} - \theta_\text{base}
$$

Add task vectors to combine capabilities. Subtract one to remove a
behavior. Average them. Scale them. Linear mode connectivity, the
empirical observation that fine-tunes from a shared base lie in a
connected low-loss basin, is the reason this works at all.

The state of the art is **TIES-Merging** and its descendants. The
problem with naive averaging is *interference*: parameters one task
moves up, another moves down. TIES:

1. **Trim**: zero out the smallest-magnitude entries of each task
   vector.
2. **Elect** a sign per parameter: the majority direction across tasks.
3. **Disjoint merge**: average only the values that agree with the
   elected sign.

The result keeps each task's important updates while resolving conflicts
deterministically. **DARE**, **SLERP**, and Arcee's mergekit recipes
build on this.

<Callout variant="insight" title="Why merging is profound">
The fact that you can blend two specialists by averaging their weights
is implicit evidence that fine-tunes occupy a connected, low-curvature
region of parameter space. The geometry that makes ensembling work for
predictions also makes merging work for weights.
</Callout>

## Synthetic data: quality over scale

Phi-1's "Textbooks Are All You Need" demonstrated that **6B
high-quality synthetic tokens** beat 100x as many web tokens for code
generation in a 1.3B model. Phi-2, Phi-3, and the open-source community
(OpenHermes, Tülu, Nemotron-mini) extended this.

The recipe is roughly:

1. Use a strong teacher to *write* training data: textbook-style
   explanations, question/answer pairs, chain-of-thought traces.
2. Filter aggressively for diversity and difficulty.
3. Train a small student on this curated corpus.

The student's quality-per-parameter goes up dramatically. Training on
"clean signal from a smart teacher" is qualitatively different from
training on "noisy signal at scale."

<Callout variant="warning" title="The synthetic-data trap">
Distillation is fundamentally bounded by the teacher's capability and
biases. A student trained only on a teacher's outputs inherits its
blindspots — and any error in the teacher gets amplified by being
treated as ground truth. Mix synthetic with real, evaluate against
held-out human-authored benchmarks, and watch for mode collapse.
</Callout>

## When to reach for which

- **Distillation**: when you have a much larger teacher and need a
  cheap, fast student of similar quality on a defined scope.
- **Merging**: when you have several fine-tunes (different tasks,
  different preference data) and want one model that does all of them
  passably.
- **Synthetic data**: when you need to train (or fine-tune) on a
  capability the teacher already has but the available real data does
  not cleanly cover.

These compose. The standard 2025 recipe for a small-but-strong open
model: distill traces from a frontier teacher → train a base → fine-tune
several specialists → TIES-merge them. Every step squeezes more
capability into the same parameter budget.
