For most of deep learning's history we have known *what* networks do —
they generalize, they hallucinate, they refuse — without knowing *how*
they do it. Mechanistic interpretability is the bet that we can change
this: that neural networks are not inscrutable, that they implement
identifiable algorithms in identifiable parts of their weights, and that
those algorithms are extractable.

This is no longer purely academic. Anthropic, OpenAI, and DeepMind have
all built dedicated interpretability teams; SAE-extracted features are
being used in production for steering and debugging; "alignment" as a
research program is increasingly inseparable from interpretability.

## Why this matters

Three reasons it matters now and will matter more.

1. **Safety**. The alternative to understanding a model is hoping it
   behaves. As models get more capable, hope is not a strategy.
2. **Debugging**. Interpretability is finally producing tools that
   localize bugs (a head that does the wrong thing, a feature that fires
   on adversarial inputs).
3. **Science**. After decades of reproducing brain-imaging-grade
   correlational claims about what networks "represent," circuits-style
   work is producing causal, mechanistic explanations.

## The picture: residual stream + circuits

The Anthropic mathematical framework recasts a Transformer as a
**residual stream** — a high-dimensional vector that every block reads
from, modifies, and writes back into. Heads and MLPs are *additive
edits*. The residual stream is the medium in which features live; heads
and MLPs are the operations that move and transform them.

Two kinds of object live there:

- **Features**: directions in the residual stream that correspond to
  human-meaningful concepts ("Golden Gate Bridge", "Python identifier",
  "negation").
- **Circuits**: subgraphs of attention heads and MLP neurons that
  implement a specific algorithm (induction, indirect object identification,
  modular addition).

Mech interp's empirical bet is that *most* of what a model does
decomposes into a manageable number of features and circuits.

## Superposition: the core obstacle

Why isn't this trivial? Because models do not store one feature per
neuron. They cannot — they have far more concepts to represent than they
have neurons in any layer.

The **superposition hypothesis** (Elhage et al., 2022) explains why a
single neuron lights up for unrelated concepts ("polysemanticity"): if
features are *sparse* (most are off most of the time), it is information-
theoretically efficient to pack many of them into the same neuron at the
cost of occasional interference. The geometry is roughly that the network
embeds a high-dimensional sparse code into the smaller available
dimension.

<Callout variant="insight" title="Why polysemantic neurons are not a bug">
A network with $d$ neurons that needs to represent $D \gg d$ features can
still do it cheaply if features rarely co-occur: assign each one an
almost-orthogonal direction in $d$-space and accept rare collisions. The
remarkable fact is that gradient descent finds these arrangements without
being told to.
</Callout>

## Sparse autoencoders: un-mixing superposition

If features are linear combinations in superposition, you can try to
recover them. Train a wide, sparse autoencoder on the residual stream
activations:

$$
z = \mathrm{ReLU}(W_e (x - b) + b_e), \qquad \hat x = W_d z + b_d
$$

with a reconstruction loss plus a sparsity penalty (typically $L_1$ on
$z$ or top-$k$). The trick is that the SAE is **over-complete**: $z$ has
many more dimensions than $x$. Sparsity forces $z$ to be a dictionary
expansion: each input activation is the sum of a few "atoms" in $W_d$.

Under the superposition hypothesis, those atoms tend to be the underlying
features. Empirically this works astonishingly well: SAEs trained on a
real LLM's residual stream produce thousands to millions of features
that humans can label — concepts, syntactic patterns, places, characters,
sentiments.

```python
import torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

class SparseAutoencoder(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d_model, d_dict, k=64):
        super().__init__()
        self.W_e = nn.Linear(d_model, d_dict, bias=False)
        self.W_d = nn.Linear(d_dict, d_model, bias=False)
        self.b_pre = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(d_model))
        self.k = k

    def forward(self, x):
        z = self.W_e(x - self.b_pre)
        # Top-k SAE: keep the k largest pre-activations, zero the rest
        topk_vals, topk_idx = z.topk(self.k, dim=-1)
        z_sparse = torch.zeros_like(z).scatter_(-1, topk_idx, F.relu(topk_vals))
        x_hat = self.W_d(z_sparse) + self.b_pre
        return x_hat, z_sparse
```

Anthropic's *Scaling Monosemanticity* paper extracted ~34M features from
Claude 3 Sonnet's middle layer. They include features that fire for
specific code idioms, specific entities, specific affective tones — and
several with concerning behavior (deception-related, sycophancy-related)
that Anthropic could *causally* steer.

## Circuits: where features connect

Features by themselves are nouns. Circuits are verbs. A celebrated
example:

- **Indirect object identification (IOI)**: the model resolves "When
  Mary and John went to the store, John gave a drink to ___" with
  "Mary" via a multi-head circuit involving previous-token heads,
  duplicate-token heads, name-mover heads, and inhibitory heads.

The Wang et al. 2022 paper reverse-engineered this circuit in GPT-2
small: every step is named, located, and ablated. Removing the circuit
breaks the behavior; the prediction is mechanistic, not statistical.

A second canonical circuit: **induction heads**. A previous-token head
writes the current token into the residual stream of the next position;
a downstream head matches that signature against the current key, and
copies the token that followed it last time. Together they implement
"if you saw `A B ... A`, predict `B`." Olsson et al. showed that
induction heads emerge sharply during training at the same moment ICL
ability emerges. Strong evidence that ICL has identifiable mechanism.

## Causal interventions: not just looking

A defining commitment of mech interp is that correlation is not enough.
The standard tools are:

- **Activation patching**: replace a head's output (or a feature's
  activation) with the corresponding value from a different prompt.
  Measure how the prediction changes. Localizes which components are
  causally responsible for which behavior.
- **Feature steering**: add $\alpha \cdot \mathbf{f}$ to the residual
  stream and see what happens. Anthropic's Golden Gate Claude clamped a
  single feature; the model insisted on bringing up the Golden Gate
  Bridge in nearly every response.
- **Path patching**: a refinement that isolates *which* downstream
  computation a given component's output flows into.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Interpretability illusions">
Linear probes and naive activation analyses produce explanations that
seem plausible but fail under intervention. If your method only tells
you "this neuron correlates with X," you don't know whether the network
*uses* X. The causal-intervention move is what separates mech interp
from older representation analysis.
</Callout>

## Where this is going

- **SAEs at scale**: Gemma Scope (DeepMind) and others have released
  per-layer SAEs trained on frontier-class models. Browsing these on
  Neuronpedia is a fast way to see the texture.
- **Cross-coders and transcoders**: extending SAEs to capture the
  *transformations* features undergo, not just their resting states.
- **Automating interpretability**: using LLMs to label features at
  scale. Quality is improving but human verification still matters.
- **Alignment applications**: deception detection, refusal-circuit
  analysis, jailbreak diagnostics — production interpretability is
  starting to ship.

## What to read next

Order matters here.

1. *A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits* — gives you the
   residual-stream and head-decomposition vocabulary.
2. *Toy Models of Superposition* — the conceptual bedrock for SAEs.
3. *Towards Monosemanticity* and *Scaling Monosemanticity* — current
   state of the art for SAE-based feature extraction.
4. ARENA's mech-interp curriculum — hands-on, with TransformerLens.

If you can extract a feature with an SAE, find a circuit that uses it,
and verify the causal role with patching, you have done original
research-quality mechanistic interpretability. The bar is not where
people think it is — there is more low-hanging fruit than people
working on it.
