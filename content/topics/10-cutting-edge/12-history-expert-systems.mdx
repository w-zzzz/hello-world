The second AI winter is the one most working researchers remember. It came
out of the *expert systems* boom of the 1980s — the most commercially
successful AI of its era — and crashed hard around 1987-1993. Knowing why
expert systems worked, then why they collapsed, is the cleanest existing
case study of what happens when a paradigm hits a structural ceiling.

It also matters because expert-systems thinking has *not* gone away. It
hides inside every modern agent that calls a calculator, every retrieval
index, every JSON schema, every Lean verifier in a reasoning loop. The
lesson was never "rules are bad"; it was "rules alone do not scale."

## DENDRAL and the birth of expert systems

DENDRAL (Stanford, 1965) is generally credited as the first expert system.
The problem: given a mass-spectrometry trace and a chemical formula, infer
the molecular structure. Edward Feigenbaum, Joshua Lederberg, and Bruce
Buchanan built a system that encoded the heuristic rules a human chemist
would use — try the most plausible substructures first, prune the search
space — and the system outperformed graduate students on its target task.

The lesson Feigenbaum drew from DENDRAL became the *knowledge principle*:
intelligence comes mostly from *knowledge of the domain*, not from
general-purpose reasoning. To make a smart AI, encode what experts know.

This was the founding bet of an entire generation.

## MYCIN, R1/XCON, and the commercial boom

MYCIN (Stanford, 1972-mid-80s) diagnosed bacterial infections and
recommended antibiotics. ~600 rules, certainty factors for uncertainty,
backward chaining inference engine. In blind evaluations MYCIN's
recommendations matched those of infectious-disease specialists. (It was
never clinically deployed, partly for liability reasons.)

R1/XCON (DEC + CMU, 1980) configured VAX computer systems. Customers
specified components; XCON produced a complete consistent configuration.
By 1986 it was reported to be saving DEC ~$25M/year. This was real,
production-scale AI generating measurable revenue.

The commercial boom that followed was significant. Symbolics and LMI
shipped specialized LISP machines — physical workstations optimized for
running LISP, which was the dominant AI programming language. By 1985 the
expert systems industry was worth billions; major corporations had
dedicated AI departments; consultants like Teknowledge and IntelliCorp
sold knowledge-engineering services.

## The structural ceiling

Three problems compounded:

1. **The knowledge acquisition bottleneck**. Each rule required eliciting
   tacit expertise from a human, formalizing it, debugging interactions
   with the existing rule base. A 600-rule system took years to build. A
   million-rule system was a multi-decade project (Cyc, founded 1984,
   still ongoing as of 2025).
2. **Brittleness at the edges**. Expert systems performed well *within*
   the carefully engineered scope of the rule base and failed
   catastrophically just outside it. They had no notion of "I don't
   know" beyond what was encoded.
3. **Maintenance cost grew super-linearly**. Adding a rule could break
   inferences that depended on the absence of that rule's hypothesis.
   Rule bases became archaeological sites.

By the late 1980s, companies that had invested heavily in expert systems
discovered that maintenance costs were eating the savings. The dedicated
LISP-machine market collapsed (1987-1990) as cheaper general-purpose
workstations from Sun and others became available. The expert-systems
industry did not survive the early 1990s in recognizable form.

## Cyc: the noble failure

Doug Lenat's **Cyc** project (started 1984 at MCC, then Cycorp) is the
most ambitious expert-systems-lineage project ever attempted. The premise:
encode all of human commonsense knowledge as logical axioms — millions of
facts about objects, agents, time, causality, social conventions — and you
will have a substrate on which intelligent reasoning becomes possible.

After four decades of effort, Cyc has accumulated millions of axioms in
several specialized ontologies. It has been used commercially in narrow
applications. It has not produced general intelligence. The project is
the cleanest existing demonstration that the *encode-everything-by-hand*
strategy does not reach commonsense competence at any feasible scale.

The data-driven approach took the opposite bet — *let the model learn
commonsense from text and images at scale* — and it worked.

## What expert systems left behind

Three technical legacies survive in modern practice:

1. **Tools and verifiers in the loop with LLMs**. A calculator the model
   can call, a Python REPL, Lean (in AlphaProof), a unit-test runner
   (in coding agents) — all are crisp rule-based systems that
   complement the learned model. This is the *productive* version of
   the expert-systems insight: hand-coded knowledge for *crisp* domains,
   learning for *fuzzy* ones.
2. **RAG and structured retrieval**. The retrieval index is a knowledge
   base. The query rewriter is a small inference engine. The pattern is
   familiar.
3. **Schema-constrained outputs**. JSON-mode, structured output, function
   schemas — declarative specifications that constrain model behavior.
   These are descendants of frame-based knowledge representation.

```python
# A modern echo of the expert-system pattern, embedded in an LLM agent.
@tool
def calculate_drug_dose(weight_kg: float, age_y: int, drug: str) -> dict:
    """Computes pediatric dosing based on hand-coded clinical rules.
    Falls through to LLM reasoning if no rule applies."""
    rule = DOSING_RULES.lookup(drug, age_y)
    if rule:
        return {"dose_mg": rule.compute(weight_kg), "source": rule.id}
    raise ToolNotApplicable("No clinical rule for this combination.")
```

This is exactly the pattern MYCIN tried to be — but as one tool among many,
called by a learned model that knows when to invoke it and when to reason
freely. The hybrid is the right answer.

## What killed the second winter

The crash bottomed around 1993. Recovery came from three sources, none of
them rule-based:

- **Statistical NLP** (IBM's translation system, late 80s) showed that
  noisy real-world tasks responded better to data than to rules.
- **Bayesian networks** (Pearl, 1988) gave a principled treatment of
  uncertainty that handled what expert-system "certainty factors" had
  bodged.
- **Neural networks** survived in pockets (CNN for digit recognition,
  LSTM for sequences) and re-emerged with deep belief nets (2006) and
  AlexNet (2012).

The third winter — should one come — will probably look different from
the previous two. But the dynamic of overclaiming, structural ceiling,
funding retreat is durable enough that it is worth knowing the historical
shape.

<Callout variant="insight" title="Why this is in a 2026 ML curriculum">
The 1980s expert-systems community made a wrong but understandable bet:
that intelligence was mostly knowledge encoded as rules. Modern LLMs
are the opposite bet: that intelligence emerges from learning over data
at scale. Both bets are partial. The systems that work best in 2026 —
agentic coding, AlphaProof, RAG-augmented chatbots — combine learned
models with hand-coded knowledge in tools and verifiers. We have
synthesized the two paradigms; we just don't always remember the
provenance.
</Callout>
