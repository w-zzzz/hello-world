A 70B model in fp16 is 140 GB. The same model in 4-bit is 35 GB. The
parameters did not change; only the data type did. Quantization is the
single highest-leverage trick for inference economics, and it is the
quietly load-bearing piece behind every "run a frontier model on consumer
hardware" demo.

## Why this matters

Inference cost scales with bytes moved more than with FLOPs. Every
generated token reads the entire model from HBM at least once; halving the
weight precision approximately halves latency on memory-bound workloads.
For long-context, KV cache is also dominated by precision. Get this right
and your serving cost drops by 4x with no architecture change.

## The precision ladder

Modern LLM stacks operate across at least four formats:

- **fp32** — historical default, nobody uses for weights anymore.
- **bf16 / fp16** — training and high-accuracy inference. 16 bits.
- **fp8 / INT8** — fast on Hopper (FP8) and most GPUs (INT8). 2x smaller.
- **INT4 / NF4** — inference-only, 4x smaller than fp16.

The general rule: you can usually drop one rung with negligible loss
post-training; dropping two needs care; dropping three (1–2 bits) needs
quantization-aware training or distillation.

## The arithmetic

Symmetric uniform quantization maps a tensor by

$$
q = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(\mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{w}{s}\right), -2^{b-1}, 2^{b-1}-1\right), \qquad \hat w = s \cdot q
$$

with scale $s$ chosen per-tensor, per-channel, or per-group (block of 64–
128 weights). Finer granularity reduces the impact of outliers but adds
metadata cost. Asymmetric variants add a zero-point.

For activations the story is harder: their distribution shifts per token,
so static per-tensor scales fail and dynamic / per-token scales are
necessary. This is what made INT8 weight-only quantization easy and INT8
activation quantization a research project.

## GPTQ: rounding with memory

Naive rounding ignores layer-level error. GPTQ formulates per-layer
quantization as

$$
\min_{\hat W} \; \| WX - \hat W X \|_F^2
$$

on a small calibration set $X$, then uses the Hessian
$H = 2 X X^\top$ to greedily quantize one column of $W$ at a time and
update the remaining columns to cancel as much of the introduced error as
possible. The result: 4-bit GPTQ recovers most of the original perplexity
that naive RTN destroys.

<Callout variant="insight" title="Why activation-aware?">
A weight matters as much as the activations it multiplies. AWQ formalizes
that: a channel with large activations is "salient." Pre-scale those
channels before quantization and you spend resolution where it pays.
</Callout>

```python
import torch
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AwqConfig

quant_cfg = AwqConfig(bits=4, group_size=128, version="gemm")
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(
    "meta-llama/Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct",
    quantization_config=quant_cfg,
    torch_dtype=torch.float16,
    device_map="auto",
)
```

## NF4 and 4-bit inference

NF4 (from QLoRA) places its 16 representable values at the quantiles of a
standard normal — so a block of weights normalized to unit variance sees
near-optimal levels. Combined with double quantization (compress the
per-block scales themselves) and a fast dequantize-then-matmul kernel,
NF4 has become a practical default for 4-bit LLM weights.

## What about activations and KV cache?

Beyond weights, two more places matter:

- **Activations**. Tools like SmoothQuant rebalance the dynamic range
  between weights and activations so both can fit INT8. FP8 on Hopper sidesteps
  most of this with a hardware-friendly format.
- **KV cache**. For long contexts the cache dwarfs the weights. INT8/FP8
  KV-cache compression (vLLM, TensorRT-LLM) is now standard.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Calibration set quality matters">
Quantization quality depends on the few hundred samples you calibrate on.
Use in-distribution data; in particular, code models calibrated on prose
will degrade on code. This is the closest "post-training quantization"
gets to fine-tuning.
</Callout>

## When to QAT

Quantization-aware training simulates quant noise during fine-tuning so
the model adapts. It is overkill for weight-only INT4 but increasingly
standard for sub-4-bit (1–2 bit) regimes such as BitNet and AQLM. Expect
QAT to matter more as the precision floor keeps dropping.
