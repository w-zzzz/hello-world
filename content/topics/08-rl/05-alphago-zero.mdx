In 2016, AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol. In 2017, AlphaGo Zero beat AlphaGo 100–0
having never seen a human game. In 2018, AlphaZero generalized the same
algorithm to chess and shogi and beat the world's strongest engines in
hours of training. The formula — Monte Carlo Tree Search guided by deep
neural networks, trained by self-play — is one of the cleanest and most
powerful ideas in modern RL.

## Why this matters

Pure model-free RL (Q-learning, policy gradients) struggles in domains
with enormous state spaces, sparse rewards, and long-horizon credit
assignment. Go has $\approx 10^{170}$ legal positions and a single
reward at the end of a multi-hundred-move game. Brute-force search is
infeasible; brute-force learning is too slow.

AlphaZero's answer: combine *learning* and *search*. The network proposes
moves and evaluates positions; tree search refines those proposals into
something stronger. The network is then trained to match the search. Each
side amplifies the other.

This is the prototype for all "system 2" reasoning approaches that came
after — including the reasoning-trace post-training that produces o1, o3,
and DeepSeek-R1.

## The architecture

A single residual ConvNet (later: Transformer-tower in MuZero) takes the
board state and outputs two heads:

- **Policy head** $p(s, \cdot)$: a probability distribution over moves.
- **Value head** $v(s)$: a scalar in $[-1, 1]$ predicting the eventual
  game outcome from the current player's perspective.

That's the whole network. No hand-crafted features. In Go, the input is
literally the stack of recent board planes.

## MCTS, with neural priors

A Monte Carlo Tree Search builds a partial game tree, focusing on
promising branches. Each node tracks $N(s, a)$ (visit count), $W(s, a)$
(total value), and $Q(s, a) = W/N$ (mean value).

Each simulation:

1. **Select.** From the root, descend by

   $$
   a^* = \arg\max_a \left[ Q(s, a) + c \cdot p(s, a) \cdot \frac{\sqrt{\sum_b N(s, b)}}{1 + N(s, a)} \right]
   $$

   The first term exploits known good moves; the second explores using
   the prior $p$ and decays as a child is visited (PUCT formula).

2. **Expand.** When you reach a leaf, evaluate it with the network to get
   $p(s_{\mathrm{leaf}}, \cdot)$ and $v(s_{\mathrm{leaf}})$.

3. **Backup.** Propagate $v$ up the path, updating each $W, N$.

After hundreds of simulations, the visit distribution $\pi(\cdot|s) \propto
N(s, \cdot)^{1/\tau}$ at the root is the MCTS policy — strictly stronger
than the raw network policy $p$, because search has explored, validated,
and refined.

## Self-play as policy iteration

Now the brilliant part. The MCTS-improved policy is the training target:

$$
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = (z - v_\theta(s))^2 - \pi^{\mathrm{MCTS}} \cdot \log p_\theta(s) + c \|\theta\|^2
$$

Three terms: a regression on the game outcome $z$, a cross-entropy on the
search-refined policy, and weight decay. The training data is generated
entirely by self-play: the current network plays against itself, MCTS
guides every move, and the resulting trajectories — with the search
distributions and final outcomes — are stored for training.

```python
def train_step(net, mcts_data, optimizer):
    # mcts_data: list of (state, mcts_policy_pi, game_outcome_z)
    states, pis, zs = batch(mcts_data)
    p_logits, v = net(states)
    policy_loss = -(pis * log_softmax(p_logits, dim=-1)).sum(dim=-1).mean()
    value_loss  = (zs - v).pow(2).mean()
    loss = policy_loss + value_loss + 1e-4 * sum(p.pow(2).sum() for p in net.parameters())
    optimizer.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); optimizer.step()
```

This is policy iteration in the classical sense:

- **Policy improvement**: MCTS turns $p_\theta$ into a stronger policy $\pi^{\mathrm{MCTS}}$.
- **Policy evaluation**: self-play games give value targets.
- **Update**: train the network to match.

Iterate until superhuman.

## What AlphaGo Zero proved

The lesson of Zero is that *human data was the bottleneck*. The 2016
AlphaGo bootstrapped from human Go games; Zero started from random play
and was stronger within 40 days. Hand-crafted features, opening books,
and supervised pretraining all turned out to be scaffolding the algorithm
didn't need.

AlphaZero generalized further: same algorithm, same hyperparameters, three
games. MuZero went further still by *learning* the dynamics model, removing
the requirement that the rules be known.

<Callout variant="insight" title="The pattern for the next decade">
The AlphaZero recipe — learned proposal + search + outcome-based update —
is the backbone of o1/o3-style reasoning models, AlphaProof's IMO results,
and AlphaCode's competitive-programming runs. The proposal becomes a
language model, the search becomes chain-of-thought rollout (sometimes with
a learned verifier instead of MCTS), and the outcome is whatever can be
checked. The algorithmic core is the same.
</Callout>

## Why it doesn't trivially port to LLMs

Search needs three things AlphaZero had and language modeling lacks:

1. A **simulator** you can rewind. Games trivially reset; conversations and
   real-world reasoning don't.
2. A **verifiable terminal reward**. Win/loss/draw is unambiguous. "Helpful
   answer" is not.
3. A tractable **branching factor**. Go has $\approx 250$ legal moves;
   language has 100,000+ token possibilities, with very long horizons.

Reasoning-model RL handles this with verifiable subdomains (math, code),
shorter rollouts, learned verifiers, and tree search of a different shape
(beam, ToT, self-consistency). The conceptual frame — search-augmented
policy improvement — is preserved.

If you understand AlphaZero, you understand the prototype every test-time
compute paper from 2024 onwards is a variation on. It is, in many ways,
the most important RL system ever built.
