Reinforcement learning is the math of *learning to make decisions*. Before
PPO, RLHF, AlphaZero — before any of the algorithms that made RL famous —
you need the underlying object they all optimize over: the **Markov
decision process**.

This is short, formal, and unavoidable. Every RL paper assumes you know it.

## Why this matters

Supervised learning gives you a label and asks you to predict it.
Reinforcement learning gives you a reward signal — possibly delayed, possibly
sparse, possibly noisy — and asks you to choose actions over time that
maximize cumulative reward. The MDP is the formalism that makes "over time"
precise.

The same structure shows up far beyond games and robots. RLHF, agent
planning, dialog management, even active learning — all of these reduce to
"choose actions in an MDP".

## The five-tuple

A (discounted, infinite-horizon) MDP is $\langle \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma \rangle$:

- $\mathcal{S}$: state space.
- $\mathcal{A}$: action space.
- $P(s' | s, a)$: transition probabilities.
- $R(s, a)$: reward for taking action $a$ in state $s$ (possibly stochastic).
- $\gamma \in [0, 1)$: discount factor.

A **policy** $\pi(a | s)$ is a (possibly stochastic) mapping from states to
actions. The agent's goal is to maximize the expected discounted return:

$$
J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_\pi \!\left[ \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t R(s_t, a_t) \right]
$$

The **Markov property** says the future depends on the present alone:
$P(s_{t+1} | s_t, a_t, s_{t-1}, \dots) = P(s_{t+1} | s_t, a_t)$. If your
problem doesn't satisfy this, fold history into the state until it does.

## Value functions

Two quantities organize all of RL:

$$
V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi \!\left[ \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t R(s_t, a_t) \,\Big|\, s_0 = s \right]
$$

$$
Q^\pi(s, a) = \mathbb{E}_\pi \!\left[ \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t R(s_t, a_t) \,\Big|\, s_0 = s, a_0 = a \right]
$$

$V^\pi$ is the value of being in state $s$ and following $\pi$ forever.
$Q^\pi$ is the value of taking one specific first action $a$ and *then*
following $\pi$. Their relationship:
$V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi(\cdot|s)}[Q^\pi(s, a)]$.

## The Bellman equations

The defining recursion of RL:

$$
V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}\!\left[ R(s, a) + \gamma \mathbb{E}_{s' \sim P}\!\left[V^\pi(s')\right] \right]
$$

This is *just the law of total expectation*: the value of $s$ equals the
immediate reward plus the discounted value of where you go next. The
**Bellman optimality equation** for the optimal value function $V^*$
replaces the policy expectation with a max:

$$
V^*(s) = \max_a \left[ R(s, a) + \gamma \mathbb{E}_{s'}[V^*(s')] \right]
$$

The corresponding optimal policy is greedy with respect to $Q^*$:
$\pi^*(s) = \arg\max_a Q^*(s, a)$.

## Two basic algorithms

When the MDP is small and known, you can solve it exactly.

```python
import numpy as np

def value_iteration(P, R, gamma=0.99, tol=1e-6):
    # P: (S, A, S')  R: (S, A)
    S, A, _ = P.shape
    V = np.zeros(S)
    while True:
        Q = R + gamma * (P * V[None, None, :]).sum(axis=-1)   # (S, A)
        V_new = Q.max(axis=-1)
        if np.abs(V_new - V).max() < tol:
            return V_new, Q.argmax(axis=-1)
        V = V_new
```

**Value iteration** repeatedly applies the Bellman optimality operator until
convergence. Because that operator is a $\gamma$-contraction in sup-norm,
the iteration converges geometrically to a unique $V^*$ regardless of
initialization.

**Policy iteration** alternates between policy evaluation (solve
$V^\pi$ for the current $\pi$) and policy improvement
($\pi'(s) = \arg\max_a Q^\pi(s, a)$). It also converges to $\pi^*$, often
faster in practice.

<Callout variant="insight" title="Why this matters past the toy regime">
Real problems don't have a known $P$ or a small state space. Q-learning is
value iteration done from samples, without ever writing down $P$. Policy
gradients are policy iteration with a learned policy network. Every
practical RL algorithm is a stochastic, function-approximated descendant of
Bellman's two equations.
</Callout>

## What to take into the next topic

Three things should be permanent furniture in your head:

1. The **state must be Markov**. If learning fails, your representation is
   often the culprit.
2. $V^\pi$ vs $Q^\pi$. Q-learning, SARSA, and policy improvement all hinge
   on $Q$, not $V$.
3. The Bellman equation is the **fixed-point equation** that every value-based
   algorithm tries to solve, exactly or approximately.

When we move to Q-learning and policy gradients, we're swapping known
dynamics for samples and tabular representations for neural networks. The
underlying object — the MDP — does not change.
