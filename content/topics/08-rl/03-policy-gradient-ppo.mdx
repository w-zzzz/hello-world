Value-based methods like Q-learning estimate $Q^*$ and act greedily. Policy
gradient methods *skip the value function* and optimize the policy
directly — by following the gradient of the expected return with respect to
the policy's parameters. They work in continuous action spaces, with
stochastic policies, and (with the right tricks) they scale to
LLM-fine-tuning, robotics, and game-playing.

PPO is the workhorse: simple enough to implement in a weekend, robust enough
to be the default RL algorithm in 2025.

## Why this matters

Q-learning is fragile in continuous control (the $\max_a Q$ becomes an
optimization), and action distributions are often more naturally modeled
than action values. Policy gradient methods accept those facts and optimize
the right object — $J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}[\sum_t \gamma^t r_t]$ —
directly.

The same machinery that trains a robot to walk also fine-tunes a language
model with RLHF. The substrate is identical.

## The policy gradient theorem

Differentiating $J(\theta)$ requires differentiating an expectation over
trajectories. The score-function trick gives a clean form:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta} \!\left[ \sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t | s_t) \cdot Q^{\pi_\theta}(s_t, a_t) \right]
$$

Sample a trajectory, compute $\nabla \log \pi$ at each step, weight by the
return-from-this-step, sum. **You never differentiate through the
environment.** That is what makes the algorithm model-free.

Two practical refinements:

1. Subtract a state-only **baseline** $b(s)$ — usually $V^\pi(s)$ — to
   reduce variance. The expected gradient is unchanged.
2. Use the **advantage** $A(s, a) = Q(s, a) - V(s)$ instead of $Q$.

This gives the **actor–critic** template: a policy network (actor) and a
value network (critic) trained jointly.

## Generalized advantage estimation

How do you estimate $A(s, a)$? Two extremes:

- $A_t = \sum_{l \ge 0} \gamma^l r_{t+l} - V(s_t)$. Monte Carlo: unbiased,
  very high variance.
- $A_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$. One-step TD: low variance, but
  biased by errors in $V$.

GAE interpolates:

$$
A_t^{\mathrm{GAE}(\gamma, \lambda)} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma \lambda)^l \, \delta_{t+l}, \quad \delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)
$$

$\lambda$ slides between TD ($\lambda = 0$) and MC ($\lambda = 1$). The
typical setting $\lambda \approx 0.95$ trades small bias for big variance
reduction.

## Trust regions and the clipping trick

Naive policy gradient is fragile because the gradient is local. One bad
update can move $\pi_\theta$ to a region where the data you collected is
worthless, and recovery is slow or impossible. **TRPO** addressed this with
a hard KL constraint:

$$
\max_\theta \; \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(a|s)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a|s)} A(s,a)\right] \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}} \Vert \pi_\theta)] \le \delta
$$

This works but requires a constrained optimizer (conjugate gradient + line
search). **PPO** replaced the constraint with a clipped surrogate:

$$
L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \min\!\big( r_t(\theta) A_t,\; \mathrm{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\, A_t \big) \right]
$$

where $r_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) / \pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_t|s_t)$
is the importance ratio and $\epsilon \approx 0.2$. The minimum-with-clip
construction means: if the new policy wants to push too far in a direction
that already increased advantage, the clip caps the gradient. A soft trust
region in one line of code.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def ppo_loss(logp_new, logp_old, advantages, returns, values, eps=0.2, vf_coef=0.5, ent_coef=0.01, entropy=None):
    ratio = torch.exp(logp_new - logp_old)
    unclipped = ratio * advantages
    clipped   = torch.clamp(ratio, 1 - eps, 1 + eps) * advantages
    policy_loss = -torch.min(unclipped, clipped).mean()
    value_loss  = F.mse_loss(values, returns)
    ent         = entropy.mean() if entropy is not None else 0.0
    return policy_loss + vf_coef * value_loss - ent_coef * ent
```

## What makes PPO actually work

The 2017 paper is short. The full implementation is not. Empirical
"implementation details" papers (notably Engstrom et al., 2020 and Huang
et al., 2022) document dozens of small choices — orthogonal initialization,
observation normalization, value clipping, learning rate annealing, advantage
normalization — that contribute as much to PPO's reputation as the clipped
objective itself.

<Callout variant="insight" title="PPO as a default">
PPO became the default not because it is theoretically optimal but because
it is a *forgiving* algorithm. It works on continuous control, discrete
action games, and LLM fine-tuning with the same hyperparameters within an
order of magnitude. That robustness is why GPT-style RLHF picked PPO and
why DeepMind, OpenAI, and Anthropic all kept variants of it as a workhorse.
</Callout>

## What comes next

The next topic — RLHF, DPO, GRPO — is in many ways a story about *replacing*
PPO. DPO eliminates the RL loop entirely; GRPO simplifies the value function.
But the algorithms are descendants. To understand why DPO is "PPO without
the value function" you have to understand PPO first.

If you can write down the policy gradient theorem, derive the advantage,
and explain why the clip ratio exists, you have the entire toolkit needed
for what comes next.
