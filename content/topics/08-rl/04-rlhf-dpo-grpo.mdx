This is the topic that connects everything you've learned about RL to the
LLM stack you actually use. Modern instruction-following models — ChatGPT,
Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek-R1, Qwen — are all post-trained on human preferences
or verifiable rewards. The algorithms have evolved fast: from PPO with a
reward model (2022), to DPO without one (2023), to GRPO (2024) and DAPO
(2025) for reasoning models.

This is also the topic where the field is moving the fastest. What was
state-of-the-art two years ago is now a baseline. Internalize the *shape* of
the problem and the algorithms will keep making sense.

## Why this matters

Pretraining gives you a base model that predicts the next token. SFT
teaches it to follow instructions. Neither tells it which of two correct
answers is *better*: more helpful, more honest, less harmful, more correctly
reasoned. Preferences fill that gap.

The technical question: how do you turn pairwise preferences (or verifiable
rewards) into a loss that improves the model? Three answers, in chronological
order, each one simpler than the last.

## RLHF: the original recipe

Christiano et al. (2017) and Stiennon et al. (2020) established the pipeline
that InstructGPT made famous:

1. **SFT.** Fine-tune the base model on demonstration data to get
   $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$.
2. **Reward model.** Collect pairwise preferences $(x, y_w, y_l)$ where $y_w$
   is preferred. Fit $r_\phi(x, y)$ under the Bradley–Terry model:

   $$
   \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{RM}}(\phi) = -\mathbb{E}\!\left[\log \sigma\!\big(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l)\big)\right]
   $$

3. **RL.** Optimize the policy with PPO against $r_\phi$, regularized by
   KL to the SFT model:

   $$
   J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_\theta}\!\left[r_\phi(x, y) - \beta \, \mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot|x) \,\Vert\, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot|x)\big)\right]
   $$

This worked, and it produced ChatGPT. But it requires *four models* in
memory at once (policy, reference, reward, critic) and a notoriously finicky
PPO loop. Reward hacking is real — the policy finds outputs that score high
on $r_\phi$ but humans hate, and the KL is the only thing preventing
catastrophic drift.

## DPO: kill the RL loop

Rafailov et al. observed that the KL-regularized RL objective has a
closed-form optimal policy:

$$
\pi^*(y | x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)} \, \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y | x) \, \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)
$$

Invert for the reward:

$$
r(x, y) \;=\; \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x)
$$

Plug this into the Bradley–Terry preference likelihood. The partition
function $Z(x)$ cancels out (it's the same for $y_w$ and $y_l$), and what
remains is a binary classification objective in $\pi_\theta$ alone:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DPO}}(\theta) \;=\; -\mathbb{E}\!\left[\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_l|x)}\right)\right]
$$

No reward model. No critic. No sampling. No PPO. Just a one-line loss on
preference triples. The two-model footprint (policy + reference) and supervised
training dynamics make DPO dramatically easier to debug.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

def dpo_loss(policy_chosen_logps, policy_rejected_logps,
             ref_chosen_logps,    ref_rejected_logps, beta=0.1):
    pi_logratios  = policy_chosen_logps  - policy_rejected_logps
    ref_logratios = ref_chosen_logps     - ref_rejected_logps
    logits = beta * (pi_logratios - ref_logratios)
    return -F.logsigmoid(logits).mean()
```

DPO is now the default in open-source post-training. Variants (IPO, KTO,
SimPO, ORPO) tweak the loss for different preference structures or to drop
the reference model.

<Callout variant="insight" title="What DPO trades">
DPO assumes the preference data is (1) static and (2) i.i.d. from a
distribution close to the SFT model. That's fine for offline preference
datasets but breaks down when you want to *iteratively improve* the policy
with on-policy samples. RL methods regain the advantage when preferences
must be elicited online or when rewards come from verifiers (math, code).
</Callout>

## GRPO: PPO without the critic

For *verifiable* rewards — math correctness, code passing tests, formatted
output — RLHF's reward-model proxy is unnecessary. You have ground truth.
But you still want online RL, because the policy needs to explore
high-quality completions that aren't in any preference dataset.

DeepSeek's **Group Relative Policy Optimization** (GRPO), introduced in
DeepSeekMath and used to train DeepSeek-R1, simplifies PPO by replacing the
critic. For each prompt $x$, sample a group of $G$ completions
$\{y_1, \dots, y_G\}$, compute their rewards, and use the *standardized
within-group reward* as the advantage:

$$
A_i \;=\; \frac{r_i - \mathrm{mean}(\{r_1, \dots, r_G\})}{\mathrm{std}(\{r_1, \dots, r_G\})}
$$

Plug this advantage into the PPO clipped objective. No value network — the
group itself provides the baseline.

The benefits compound: one fewer model in memory, no value-function bias
to chase, and on long-horizon completions (which is where chain-of-thought
RL needs to work) GRPO's group baseline is more stable than a learned
critic that has to fit a noisy value target across thousands of tokens.
GRPO is the algorithm behind DeepSeek-R1's RL stage and is now widely used
in open reasoning models.

## DAPO and the 2025 frontier

ByteDance's **DAPO** (March 2025) is the latest open-source reasoning-RL
recipe. It builds on GRPO with four important fixes:

1. **Clip-Higher.** Use asymmetric clip thresholds so the upper clip doesn't
   suppress exploration of high-reward but low-probability tokens — a real
   pathology in long-form reasoning.
2. **Dynamic sampling.** Filter prompts with all-correct or all-wrong groups
   (zero gradient signal) and oversample the informative ones.
3. **Token-level loss.** Average loss over tokens rather than over sequences,
   so a single very long completion doesn't dominate the gradient.
4. **Overlong reward shaping.** Penalize truncated completions softly so
   the policy doesn't learn to ramble until cutoff.

DAPO trained Qwen2.5-32B to 50 AIME points with fewer steps than GRPO. The
broader lesson: RL post-training for reasoning is a subfield with its own
fast-moving algorithmic landscape, all built on the PPO scaffold.

<Callout variant="warning" title="The 2025 picture">
A pragmatic stack today looks like: SFT → DPO (or its variants) for general
helpfulness → GRPO/DAPO for verifiable reasoning rewards. RLHF with a
trained reward model is still used at frontier labs — particularly Anthropic
and Google — because it captures preferences that aren't easily verifiable.
"DPO replaces RLHF" is too strong. "DPO replaces RLHF for the easy half"
is closer.
</Callout>

## What to internalize

Three pictures:

1. **RLHF**: humans rank pairs → reward model → PPO with KL penalty. Four
   models, one finicky pipeline.
2. **DPO**: solve the KL-regularized RL problem in closed form, eliminate
   the reward model, optimize the policy with binary cross-entropy on
   preference pairs.
3. **GRPO/DAPO**: when rewards are verifiable, skip the reward model
   entirely; sample groups of completions and use within-group baselines
   instead of a value network.

Every alignment paper for the rest of the decade will be a remix of these
three ideas — adding online preferences, mixing verifiable and judgmental
rewards, sharpening the loss for reasoning. If you understand why DPO
collapses RLHF and why GRPO eliminates the critic, you have the conceptual
toolkit to read the next breakthrough as it appears.
