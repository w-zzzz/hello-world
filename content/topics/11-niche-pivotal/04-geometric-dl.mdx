Most of deep learning is built on grids: image pixels, audio samples,
token sequences. But much of the world isn't gridded — molecules,
proteins, social networks, 3D point clouds, particle systems. **Geometric
deep learning** is the program of designing architectures that respect
the structure and symmetries of these domains, instead of pretending
they are images.

The payoff is concrete and large: AlphaFold's protein-structure
revolution, near-quantum-chemistry-quality molecular potentials, drug
discovery, social network analysis. Every one of these uses
architectures that respect a specific symmetry.

## Why this matters

A model that knows the symmetries of its data needs less data to learn,
generalizes better off-distribution, and is more interpretable. CNNs
beat MLPs on images because translation equivariance was baked in.
GNNs and SE(3)-equivariant networks do the same for graphs and 3D
structures. Choosing the right inductive bias is, after scale, the
single highest-leverage decision in modeling a structured domain.

## Equivariance: the unifying idea

Let $G$ be a group of transformations (translations, rotations,
permutations). A function $f$ is **equivariant** to $G$ if

$$
f(g \cdot x) = g \cdot f(x) \qquad \forall g \in G
$$

That is, applying the symmetry to the input has the same effect as
applying it to the output. **Invariance** is the special case where
the right side is just $f(x)$.

The geometric deep-learning thesis (Bronstein et al.) is that nearly
every successful deep architecture can be derived as the *most general
equivariant function* for some group:

- **CNNs** = translation-equivariant on grids.
- **Spherical CNNs** = rotation-equivariant on the sphere.
- **GNNs** = permutation-equivariant on graphs.
- **Transformers** = permutation-equivariant on sets (then add
  positional encoding).
- **DeepSets / PointNet** = permutation-invariant on sets.
- **EGNNs / NequIP / SE(3)-Transformer** = E(n) or SE(3)-equivariant on
  point clouds in $\mathbb{R}^d$.

A surprising amount of architectural progress has come from "what
symmetry am I missing?"

## Graph neural networks

Graphs are the canonical irregular structure. A GNN's job is to compute
node representations that depend on the local neighborhood, in a way
that is invariant to how the nodes are *named* (permutation-equivariant).

The standard message-passing formulation: at layer $l$,

$$
h_i^{(l+1)} = \phi\!\left( h_i^{(l)}, \; \bigoplus_{j \in N(i)} \psi(h_i^{(l)}, h_j^{(l)}, e_{ij}) \right)
$$

where $\bigoplus$ is a permutation-invariant aggregator (sum, mean,
max, attention). $\psi$ computes the message from neighbor $j$ to node
$i$; $\phi$ updates the node representation.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch_scatter

class MPNN(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d):
        super().__init__()
        self.msg = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(2 * d, d), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(d, d))
        self.upd = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(2 * d, d), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(d, d))

    def forward(self, h, edge_index):  # edges = (2, E)
        src, dst = edge_index
        m = self.msg(torch.cat([h[src], h[dst]], dim=-1))
        agg = torch_scatter.scatter_sum(m, dst, dim=0, dim_size=h.shape[0])
        return self.upd(torch.cat([h, agg], dim=-1))
```

Different aggregators, different attention, different normalizations
give you the GCN/GAT/GraphSAGE/GIN family. They differ in expressive
power: the Weisfeiler-Lehman test bounds what message-passing GNNs can
distinguish; GIN is the maximum expressive 1-WL design.

<Callout variant="insight" title="Permutation equivariance is non-negotiable">
A graph has no canonical node ordering. If your model's predictions
depend on the order, you've encoded an arbitrary choice. Sum, mean, and
attention aggregations are permutation-invariant; transformers on
sets work for the same reason.
</Callout>

## SE(3) equivariance: the AlphaFold ingredient

For molecules and proteins, the atoms live in $\mathbb{R}^3$. A rigid
translation or rotation of the molecule should give a rotated-and-
translated output (forces, structures, energies). Networks that respect
this exactly include **EGNN**, **NequIP**, **MACE**, and the
**SE(3)-Transformer**.

The key trick is to update positions and features in ways that mix
correctly under SE(3). For EGNN:

$$
\mathbf{x}_i^{l+1} = \mathbf{x}_i^l + \frac{1}{|N(i)|} \sum_{j \in N(i)} (\mathbf{x}_i^l - \mathbf{x}_j^l) \, \phi_x(m_{ij})
$$

The position update is a sum of *relative* vectors weighted by scalars
— manifestly SE(3)-equivariant. Feature updates use only invariant
quantities (distances, scalars). The network respects rotation and
translation by construction; you cannot break it by feeding rotated
inputs.

## When equivariance pays off

Equivariance is not free — it constrains the function class. The win
shows up when:

- Data is *limited* relative to the symmetry group's size. Augmentation
  can mimic some symmetries but is sample-inefficient.
- Generalization to *unseen* group elements matters (a molecule in a
  rotation never seen at training).
- Physical correctness is required (energy conservation, force
  symmetry).

For very large datasets and unconstrained problems (raw web text), the
cost of constraining the model often exceeds the benefit. This is why
plain transformers — which only enforce permutation equivariance — beat
all the symmetry-rich variants on language.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Approximate equivariance is often a trap">
Encoding equivariance approximately (data augmentation, symmetric loss
functions) gives you something that *behaves* equivariantly *on average*
but fails on rare configurations. For physics, structural biology, and
anywhere correctness matters, prefer architectures that respect the
symmetry exactly.
</Callout>

## Where it ships

- **AlphaFold 2/3** — equivariant attention over residues and atoms.
- **GNoME** (DeepMind) — graph networks for materials discovery,
  millions of new stable crystals.
- **Drug discovery** — equivariant models for binding-affinity
  prediction.
- **Particle physics** and **astronomy** — GNNs over event/object
  graphs.

## What to read next

- Bronstein et al.'s book-length survey is the comprehensive resource.
- Kipf & Welling's GCN paper for the canonical message-passing example.
- The EGNN paper for a clean, minimal SE(3)-equivariant design.
- AlphaFold 2 and 3 for the most consequential geometric DL deployment.

If your data has structure you can name — rotations, permutations, a
graph — start with an architecture that respects it. The symmetry tax
is real and so is the compounded sample-efficiency win.
