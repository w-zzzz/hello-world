What does an infinitely wide neural network look like? It turns out that
in a specific parameterization, training one is *exactly* kernel
regression. The kernel — the **Neural Tangent Kernel** — is a closed-form
function determined by the architecture, and the trained network's
predictions on test inputs have an analytical form.

NTK theory is the cleanest theoretical bridge between deep learning and
classical statistical learning. It explains real phenomena (spectral
bias, why training converges, why wide networks generalize). It also
fails to explain others (feature learning, transfer learning, scaling).
Knowing where the theory applies and where it breaks down is the point.

## Why this matters

Deep learning works much better than VC bounds say it should. Theorists
have spent two decades trying to explain this. NTK is one of the few
results that is mathematically rigorous, derives a tractable model, and
makes specific predictions that match experiments. Even where it falls
short, what falls short is informative.

## The setup

Consider an MLP with parameters $\theta$ and output $f_\theta(x)$. Use
the NTK parameterization (a specific scaling of weights and learning
rates with width). Take the width $\to \infty$.

At initialization, $f_\theta(x)$ is a Gaussian process. So far, classical
infinite-width-network folklore.

The new piece is what happens *during training*. With small enough
learning rate (gradient flow), parameters change very little in
*relative* terms; the function evolves *linearly* in parameter space:

$$
f_\theta(x) \approx f_{\theta_0}(x) + \nabla_\theta f_{\theta_0}(x)^\top (\theta - \theta_0)
$$

Differentiate the gradient-flow ODE through this linearization. The
result: $f_\theta(x_\text{test})$ at convergence equals the kernel
regression prediction with kernel

$$
\Theta(x, x') = \langle \nabla_\theta f_{\theta_0}(x), \nabla_\theta f_{\theta_0}(x') \rangle
$$

evaluated at initialization. This is the **NTK**. For each architecture
(MLP, CNN, ResNet) it has a closed-form recursion through the layers.

## What this explains

### Spectral bias

Decompose the targets in the NTK eigenbasis: $y = \sum_i c_i \phi_i$,
where $\phi_i$ are eigenfunctions of the integral operator with kernel
$\Theta$. Under gradient flow, the residual decays component-wise:

$$
r_i(t) = c_i \, e^{-\lambda_i t}
$$

High-eigenvalue (smooth) components are learned fast; low-eigenvalue
(wiggly, high-frequency) components are learned slowly. This **spectral
bias** is observable in real networks: they fit low-frequency
structure first.

### Convergence

The NTK Gram matrix on the training set is positive definite under mild
conditions. Gradient descent on MSE loss converges linearly to zero
training loss at the rate of its smallest eigenvalue. This is why
massively over-parameterized networks reliably reach zero training
loss.

### Initialization scaling

NTK derivations make the right initialization scales explicit (LeCun,
Glorot, He init are all NTK-flavored). They tell you exactly how to
scale weights so the forward pass and gradients don't blow up with
depth.

```python
# A high-level sketch — actual NTK computation uses recursive activation
# moment formulas; libraries like neural-tangents do this efficiently.
import jax, jax.numpy as jnp
from neural_tangents import stax

init_fn, apply_fn, kernel_fn = stax.serial(
    stax.Dense(512), stax.Relu(),
    stax.Dense(512), stax.Relu(),
    stax.Dense(1),
)
K_train = kernel_fn(x_train, x_train, "ntk")          # NTK Gram matrix
K_test  = kernel_fn(x_test,  x_train, "ntk")
y_pred  = K_test @ jnp.linalg.solve(K_train + 1e-4 * jnp.eye(len(x_train)), y_train)
```

The same network, trained at infinite width, gives the same predictions
as this kernel regression. You can sanity-check NTK predictions against
real wide networks experimentally.

<Callout variant="insight" title="The lazy regime">
Chizat & Bach named this the *lazy regime*: parameters barely move
relative to their initial scale. Features (the network's intermediate
activations) are essentially frozen at their random-initialization
form; only the readout layer effectively adapts. Useful theoretically.
Diametrically opposed to what makes pretraining valuable.
</Callout>

## What it does not explain

NTK predicts that infinite-width networks learn *no features*. They
behave like fixed-feature kernel machines. But empirically:

- Pretraining matters enormously — there is a real benefit from learned
  features.
- Transfer learning works — the features that pretraining produces are
  reusable across tasks.
- Scaling laws show that more parameters and more data improve
  capability in ways that NTK's lazy-regime prediction does not capture.

Yang's **Tensor Programs** and the **µP** parameterization show that the
infinite-width limit is not unique: under different scaling, you get
different limits, some of which **do** feature-learn. NTK is one
specific (and lazy) corner.

## When to actually care about NTK

- Designing initialization and learning-rate schedules. The math forces
  you to get scales right.
- Predicting which architectures will train well — small-curvature
  properties of NTK transfer to finite-width regimes.
- Understanding spectral bias and why networks struggle with
  high-frequency targets (relevant for implicit-neural-representation
  work).
- Reasoning about kernel-regression baselines as a lower bound on what
  a deep network is doing.

If you walk away with one thing: deep learning is *not* kernel
regression in practice, but it sometimes behaves like it, and
understanding when and why is one of the few solid theoretical
footholds we have.
