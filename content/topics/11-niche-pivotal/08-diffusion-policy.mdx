Diffusion did not just take over image generation. By 2023-2024 it had
become the dominant policy class for robot visuomotor control, and it sits
at the heart of every serious robot-learning foundation model in 2026.
This module is about why — what makes diffusion the right policy class
when the action distribution is multimodal — and how the idea connects to
both classical RL and the VLA models in part 6.

It is short and dense because the technical content is mostly familiar
from part 6 (DDPM/flow matching) and part 8 (policy gradients). The new
material is the *connection* and what it lets you do in the action space.

## The motivating problem: multimodality

Imitation learning from human demonstrations is the dominant data source
for robot manipulation. The fundamental difficulty: human demonstrations
are *multimodal*. Two demonstrators showing "pour water into the cup"
will reach for the bottle with different trajectories — left arm or
right arm, around the obstacle one way or the other, fast or slow.

A standard Gaussian-output policy regresses to the *mean* of these
demonstrations. The mean of "go left" and "go right" is "go straight
through the obstacle." This failure mode is endemic in behavior cloning;
it is one of the central reasons imitation learning has historically been
fragile.

The classical fixes — Gaussian mixtures, autoregressive
discretization (RT-1, BC-Z) — work but each has costs. GMMs are hard
to train stably; discretization fragments the action space and loses
precision on continuous control.

## Diffusion as a flexible action distribution

A diffusion model is a *very* flexible distribution. It can represent
arbitrarily multimodal action densities, including modes of different
masses and shapes. Conditioning on observations (camera images,
proprioception, language instruction) gives a conditional distribution
$p(a_{1:H} \mid o)$ that captures whichever modes the data exhibits.

The recipe (Chi et al. 2023):

1. Each training example is $(o, a_{1:H})$ — observation and a chunk of
   $H$ subsequent actions.
2. Add noise to the action chunk: $a_{1:H}^{(k)} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_k}\, a_{1:H} + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_k}\, \varepsilon$.
3. Train a network $\varepsilon_\theta(a_{1:H}^{(k)}, o, k)$ to predict
   the noise.
4. At inference: sample $a_{1:H}^{(K)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, then
   denoise iteratively conditioned on the current observation, execute
   the first few actions, replan with a new observation, repeat.

```python
# Conceptual training step for Diffusion Policy.
o, a_chunk = sample_demo()             # (obs, action chunk of length H)
k = randint(1, K)                      # noise step
noise = randn_like(a_chunk)
a_noisy = sqrt(abar[k]) * a_chunk + sqrt(1 - abar[k]) * noise
loss = mse(eps_net(a_noisy, o, k), noise)
```

The architectural choices that work best — at least in 2023-25 — are
1D-conv U-Nets or transformer denoisers on the action chunk dimension,
with FiLM-style conditioning on observations. Sampling uses DDIM or DPM-Solver
to keep the inference cost down to ~10 denoising steps.

## Why action chunking matters

The other half of Diffusion Policy's recipe is **action chunking**: predict
$H$ future actions ($H \approx 8\text{-}16$) and execute them open-loop
before replanning. Chunking helps for several reasons:

- **Temporal coherence.** Single-step policies introduce per-step jitter;
  chunked predictions are smoother and more demonstration-like.
- **Implicit lookahead.** The chunk captures short-horizon dependencies
  ("first lift, then rotate, then place") that a per-step policy must
  re-derive.
- **Reactive replanning.** Re-querying every $H/2$ steps balances
  reactivity with smoothness. The policy is not committed to a stale
  plan if the world changes.

The right $H$ is task-dependent (smaller for contact-rich, larger for
free-motion) but the principle is universal: predict more than one step,
re-plan often.

## Diffuser, planning, and goal conditioning

Janner et al.'s **Diffuser** (2022) makes a subtly different bet: use
diffusion to model the joint distribution over *trajectories*
(state-action sequences), then do classifier-guided sampling to bias
toward high-reward trajectories. This is *planning as inference* — the
model is the world; the sampler is the planner.

Diffuser is more general (it can plan against new reward functions
without retraining) but harder to train (needs a learned dynamics model).
Diffusion Policy is simpler (pure imitation) and has won more adoption,
but the two ideas combine elegantly: a learned dynamics model plus a
diffusion-policy proposal yields a powerful planning system.

Goal conditioning is where diffusion really shines. Sample-time
guidance (classifier-free guidance from text-to-image, gradient guidance
from a goal classifier, or constraint-projection from collision avoidance)
all transfer naturally to action diffusion. Plan around obstacles,
toward a goal pose, with arbitrary safety constraints — all without
retraining.

## The connection to flow matching

Flow matching (covered in part 6 topic 4) is the natural successor to
diffusion for action generation. Single-step generation, straighter
trajectories, and easier to train at scale. Physical Intelligence's
**π_0** uses flow matching as the action head precisely for this reason:
a 50 Hz control loop cannot wait for 50 denoising steps, but it can wait
for one flow-matching step.

The 2025-26 trend is clearly toward flow-matching action heads on top of
VLM backbones. Diffusion remains conceptually central — flow matching is
a continuous-time generalization of the same idea — but production systems
are largely converging on the cheaper sampler.

## How this fits the rest of the curriculum

Three places this module connects:

- **Part 6 / Diffusion** gives the theoretical machinery (forward noising,
  reverse denoising, score matching). Action diffusion just swaps "image"
  for "action chunk."
- **Part 6 / VLA** gives the architectural pattern (VLM backbone +
  action head). Diffusion is one choice of action head; π_0's
  flow-matching head is its successor.
- **Part 8 / RL** gives the alternative paradigm (policy gradients,
  reward maximization). Diffusion policies are *imitation*; RL
  fine-tuning of diffusion policies (Diffusion-DPO for control,
  AWR-style approaches) is the bridge to optimization beyond the
  demonstration distribution.
