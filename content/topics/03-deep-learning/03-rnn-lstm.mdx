Before Transformers, sequences belonged to recurrent networks. RNNs, LSTMs,
and GRUs were the workhorses of language modeling, machine translation,
speech recognition, and sequential decision-making for the better part of a
decade. They are, broadly, no longer the right answer for new
sequence-modeling problems — but the *reasons* they failed at scale led
directly to attention, and the *gating mechanisms* they invented power
modern state-space models like Mamba.

You can't fully understand why Transformers won without understanding what
they replaced.

## Why this matters

Three reasons RNNs still earn space in your head:

1. The vanishing/exploding gradient analysis that justified LSTMs is the
   same analysis that justifies residual connections in deep nets, and the
   same analysis that motivates structured state-space models.
2. **Gating** — multiplicative on/off control of information flow — is one
   of the most important architectural primitives in deep learning. It
   shows up in LSTMs, GRUs, highway networks, GLU, Mixture-of-Experts
   routers, and Mamba.
3. RNNs remain useful for streaming/online inference where memory and
   latency matter — speech models, time series, embedded systems.

## A vanilla RNN

Given inputs $x_1, \dots, x_T$ and a hidden state $h_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$:

$$
h_t = \tanh(W_{hh} h_{t-1} + W_{xh} x_t + b)
$$

The output at each step (or only at the end, depending on the task) is a
linear projection of $h_t$. Training is **backprop through time** (BPTT):
unroll the recurrence into a feed-forward graph of length $T$ and apply
standard backprop.

The trouble is in that unrolling. The gradient $\partial L / \partial h_0$
includes a product of $T$ copies of the recurrent Jacobian $\partial h_t /
\partial h_{t-1}$. If its largest eigenvalue is less than 1, the gradient
vanishes exponentially. If greater than 1, it explodes. Bengio, Simard, and
Frasconi made this rigorous in 1994: long-range learning with vanilla RNNs
is, generically, mathematically hard.

<Callout variant="info" title="Two practical fixes that always apply">
1. **Gradient clipping**: cap $\|\nabla\|$ to some threshold (e.g. 1.0).
   Doesn't fix vanishing, but prevents exploding from killing training.
2. **Truncated BPTT**: only unroll for $K$ steps (typically 32–256) before
   detaching the graph. Reduces compute and limits gradient horizons.
</Callout>

## LSTM: the constant error carousel

Hochreiter and Schmidhuber's 1997 LSTM solved vanishing gradients by adding
a separate **cell state** $C_t$ that is updated *additively* through gates.
For input $x_t$ and previous state $(h_{t-1}, C_{t-1})$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f_t &= \sigma(W_f [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_f) & \text{forget gate} \\
i_t &= \sigma(W_i [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_i) & \text{input gate} \\
o_t &= \sigma(W_o [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_o) & \text{output gate} \\
\tilde{C}_t &= \tanh(W_c [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_c) & \text{candidate} \\
C_t &= f_t \odot C_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde{C}_t & \text{cell update} \\
h_t &= o_t \odot \tanh(C_t) & \text{hidden output}
\end{aligned}
$$

The crucial line is $C_t = f_t \odot C_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde{C}_t$. The
cell state is updated by *adding* something — not multiplying — so the
recurrent Jacobian along the cell path is close to the identity whenever
the forget gate is open. Gradients flow back through time without
exponential decay. Hochreiter called this the "constant error carousel".

remains the cleanest visual treatment. Read it.

## GRU: a simpler gate

The Gated Recurrent Unit (Cho et al., 2014) merges the LSTM's input and
forget gates into one update gate, drops the output gate, and works with a
single hidden state:

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_t &= \sigma(W_z [h_{t-1}, x_t]) \\
r_t &= \sigma(W_r [h_{t-1}, x_t]) \\
\tilde{h}_t &= \tanh(W_h [r_t \odot h_{t-1}, x_t]) \\
h_t &= (1 - z_t) \odot h_{t-1} + z_t \odot \tilde{h}_t
\end{aligned}
$$

Fewer parameters, similar performance on most benchmarks. In practice, GRUs
are slightly faster to train; LSTMs win marginally on very long sequences.
Pick whichever your framework defaults to.

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

rnn = nn.LSTM(input_size=128, hidden_size=256, num_layers=2,
              bidirectional=False, batch_first=True)
x = torch.randn(8, 100, 128)               # (batch, seq, features)
y, (h, c) = rnn(x)
print(y.shape, h.shape, c.shape)           # (8, 100, 256), (2, 8, 256), (2, 8, 256)
```

## Why Transformers won

Three reasons RNNs lost language modeling:

1. **Sequential computation.** $h_t$ depends on $h_{t-1}$, so you cannot
   compute timesteps in parallel during training. Transformers process the
   whole sequence in one batched matmul.
2. **Path length.** In an RNN, information from token 1 reaches token $T$
   through $T$ recurrent steps, each lossy. In a Transformer, every token
   attends directly to every other in $O(1)$ hops.
3. **Hardware fit.** Modern GPUs are matrix-multiply machines. Transformers
   are matrix multiplies. RNN cells are small per-step operations that
   under-use the device.

The first reason is the killer. Faster training → more data and parameters
→ better models. Transformer scaling crushed RNN scaling decisively after
GPT-2 / GPT-3.

<Callout variant="insight" title="The gate idea didn't die">
Selective state-space models (Mamba, S5) reintroduce gating with carefully
chosen parameterizations of recurrent dynamics. They achieve linear-time
inference (RNN's old advantage) while preserving long-range gradient flow
(LSTM's contribution) and parallelizable training (Transformer's
contribution). The lineage is direct.
</Callout>

## What to do next

If you'll work on language modeling, you can mostly skip RNNs in practice
and jump to Transformers. But understand the LSTM gating equations once —
they're the prototype for almost every "selective" computation in modern
deep learning, and they show up unchanged in Mamba's ablations.
