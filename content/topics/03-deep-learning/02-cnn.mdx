Before Transformers ate computer vision, convolutional networks owned it.
And while ViTs now match or beat ConvNets on large-scale image
classification, the ideas that drove CNNs — locality, weight sharing,
hierarchical receptive fields, residual connections — are baked into how
the rest of deep learning thinks about structured data.

You'll still meet CNNs constantly: as the vision encoder in CLIP and
LLaVA, as the U-Net spine of every diffusion model, as detection backbones,
audio front-ends, and protein structure modules. They are not legacy.

## Why this matters

A fully connected MLP applied to a $224 \times 224 \times 3$ image needs
about 150,000 parameters per hidden unit and ignores the obvious fact that
nearby pixels are related. Convolutions enforce two priors that fix this:

- **Locality.** Useful patterns (edges, textures, parts) are local; you
  don't need to look across the whole image to detect them.
- **Translation equivariance.** A cat detector should fire on a cat
  regardless of where the cat is in the image.

Hard-coding these priors is what makes CNNs sample-efficient on visual
tasks. ViTs eventually learn similar inductive biases from data — but they
need a lot more of it.

## What a convolution actually does

A 2D convolution slides a small **kernel** $K \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times k}$ over an
input feature map $X$, producing an output where each spatial position is a
weighted sum over the local $k \times k$ patch:

$$
Y_{i, j} = \sum_{u=0}^{k-1} \sum_{v=0}^{k-1} K_{u, v} \cdot X_{i+u, j+v}
$$

Each layer typically has many filters (channels), so a convolution layer
maps $C_\text{in}$ input channels to $C_\text{out}$ output channels with
$k \times k \times C_\text{in} \times C_\text{out}$ parameters — *independent
of spatial size*. That's the weight-sharing win.

Three knobs control output geometry: **kernel size** $k$, **stride** $s$
(how far the kernel jumps each step), and **padding** $p$ (zeros added to
the border). The output height is $\lfloor (H + 2p - k)/s \rfloor + 1$.
Memorize that formula.

```python
import torch.nn as nn

block = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Conv2d(3, 64, kernel_size=7, stride=2, padding=3),  # downsample 2x
    nn.BatchNorm2d(64),
    nn.ReLU(inplace=True),
    nn.MaxPool2d(kernel_size=3, stride=2, padding=1),       # downsample 2x more
)
# Input (B, 3, 224, 224) -> output (B, 64, 56, 56). The classic ResNet stem.
```

## Hierarchy of receptive fields

A single $3 \times 3$ convolution sees only a tiny patch. Stacking them
grows the **receptive field** — the region of the input that influences a
given output position. Two stacked $3 \times 3$ layers see a $5 \times 5$
patch; three stacked see $7 \times 7$. Going deep buys you global context
through repeated local computation.

This hierarchy maps onto a feature hierarchy: early layers learn edges and
oriented gradients, middle layers learn textures and motifs, deep layers
learn parts and objects. Distill's [Feature Visualization](https://distill.pub/2017/feature-visualization/)
shows this with hallucinatory clarity.

<Callout variant="insight" title="Why small kernels stacked beat one big kernel">
Two $3\times 3$ layers have $2 \cdot 9 = 18$ parameters but the same
$5\times 5$ receptive field as a single layer with 25 parameters — and
they include a nonlinearity in between, increasing expressiveness. This
is the central design choice of VGG and basically every CNN since.
</Callout>

## Pooling and downsampling

To build hierarchy you need to reduce spatial resolution while increasing
channels. Two ways:

- **Pooling layers** — max-pool or average-pool over $2 \times 2$ windows
  with stride 2. Parameter-free.
- **Strided convolution** — convolve with stride 2 to downsample while
  learning the downsampling filter. Modern nets prefer this.

The classic pyramid: each downsampling step halves height/width and roughly
doubles channels. Total compute stays roughly constant per stage, but
spatial detail compresses into channel diversity.

## ResNets and the depth revolution

Up to about 20 layers, deeper CNNs trained better. Past that, plain stacks
got *worse* — not from overfitting, but from optimization failure.
Gradients shrank, and training loss itself stopped decreasing.

Kaiming He's 2015 fix was almost embarrassingly simple. Replace each block
$y = F(x)$ with

$$
y = x + F(x)
$$

The **residual connection** lets gradients flow unchanged through the
identity path. The block is now learning the *deviation from identity*
rather than the full mapping, which turns out to be much easier to optimize.
ResNets immediately scaled to 152 layers and crushed ImageNet 2015.

<PaperCard title="Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition" authors="He, Zhang, Ren, Sun" year={2016} url="https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385" />

The residual idea generalized far beyond CNNs. Every Transformer block uses
it. Every diffusion U-Net uses it. It is, alongside backprop and attention,
one of the few ideas that you'll meet in every architecture for the rest of
the curriculum.

## BatchNorm and friends

Each layer's input distribution shifts as upstream parameters update —
"internal covariate shift". **BatchNorm** standardizes activations across
the batch dimension, which empirically stabilizes training and lets you use
much higher learning rates. The actual mechanism is debated (smoother loss
landscape? implicit regularization? something else?), but the empirical
benefits are real.

For Transformers and small-batch settings, **LayerNorm** (normalize across
features within each example) replaced BatchNorm. **GroupNorm** is a middle
ground useful in vision when batch sizes are small.

## Modern CNNs and the ViT era

Architectures like ConvNeXt, EfficientNet, and RegNet show that careful
CNN design can match Vision Transformers on most benchmarks. The recipe
ends up being: depthwise separable convolutions (cheap), large kernels
(7×7 or 11×11 — recovering global context), inverted bottlenecks
(MobileNetV2), GELU activations, LayerNorm.

ViTs win at very large scale; CNNs win at small/medium scale and in
deployment-constrained settings. Both will keep coexisting.

<Callout variant="info" title="What to remember">
1. Convolution = locality + weight sharing + translation equivariance.
2. Stack small kernels for cheap, deep receptive fields.
3. Residual connections are how you train past ~20 layers.
4. Pooling vs. strided conv is a design choice; the rest of the
   architecture is a stack of (conv, norm, activation, residual).
</Callout>
