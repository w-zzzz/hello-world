For five years, the dominant story of LLM progress was: bigger model, more
data, better answers. In late 2024, OpenAI's o1 quietly changed the axis.
Instead of scaling parameters, scale the *amount of compute the model spends
thinking before it answers*. Then in early 2025, DeepSeek-R1 showed that
this capability could be elicited entirely through reinforcement learning —
no chain-of-thought demonstrations required. Both results upended the
field's model of where progress comes from.

This is arguably the most important shift since the original GPT-3 paper.
You should understand it precisely.

## Two compute axes

Until 2024, "more compute" meant "bigger model, more training tokens."
Inference was the cheap part. A reasoning model breaks this assumption: a
single response from o1 might involve generating 10,000+ hidden reasoning
tokens before producing a 200-token answer. That's 50× the inference cost
of a vanilla GPT-4 response on the same prompt.

The trade-off is favorable when the problem warrants it. Snell et al. (2024)
showed that on hard math and reasoning benchmarks, a small model with
generous test-time compute often outperforms a much larger model that
answers immediately, *at equal total FLOPs*. Reasoning is fungible with
parameters — to a point.

$$
\text{quality} \approx f(\underbrace{N}_{\text{params}},\ \underbrace{D}_{\text{train data}},\ \underbrace{T_{\text{think}}}_{\text{test-time tokens}})
$$

Pre-o1 we optimized $(N, D)$ with $T_{\text{think}}$ pinned near zero.
Post-o1 we let $T_{\text{think}}$ grow — and discovered the curve in that
direction was steep and far from saturated.

## How o1 was trained (and what we don't know)

OpenAI hasn't published full details. What's been confirmed:

- A *large-scale RL* algorithm trains the model to produce long internal
  chains of thought before answering.
- The reward function is a mix of automated graders (verifiable correctness
  on math, code, and other domains where right answers exist) and learned
  reward models.
- Inference time scales with problem difficulty — the model "decides" how
  much to think.

What's unconfirmed: the specific RL algorithm, the reward composition, and
the exact dataset. The community filled in the blanks via DeepSeek-R1.

## DeepSeek-R1 and GRPO: the open recipe

DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025) is the most consequential open-weights model
release of the decade. The paper described an end-to-end recipe — and
showed that a key surprise was achievable.

**R1-Zero**: starting from DeepSeek-V3 base, train *with RL only*, using
verifiable correctness as the reward (math has known answers; code passes
or fails tests). Long chain-of-thought reasoning, self-verification, and
backtracking *emerged* during training. No SFT step, no human-written CoT
data.

**R1**: the production model. Add a small SFT cold start for readability
(R1-Zero's reasoning was correct but ugly), then RL, then more SFT, then
RL again. The result matches o1 on most public reasoning benchmarks.

The RL algorithm is **GRPO** (Group Relative Policy Optimization), introduced
in the DeepSeekMath paper. Conceptually:

1. For each prompt $q$, sample $K$ completions $\{o_1, \ldots, o_K\}$ from the current policy.
2. Score them with the reward function: $r_1, \ldots, r_K$.
3. Compute group-relative advantages: $\hat{A}_i = (r_i - \bar{r}) / \sigma_r$.
4. Update the policy with a clipped PPO-style objective using $\hat{A}_i$:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}} = \mathbb{E}_q\, \frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K} \min\!\Big(\rho_i \hat{A}_i,\ \mathrm{clip}(\rho_i, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) \hat{A}_i\Big) - \beta\, D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}})
$$

where $\rho_i = \pi_\theta(o_i|q) / \pi_{\text{old}}(o_i|q)$. The crucial
innovation: **no value/critic network** is needed. The group baseline
replaces it. This halves the GPU memory footprint of RL training.

```python
# Sketch of GRPO advantage computation
def grpo_advantages(rewards):
    # rewards: tensor of shape (B, K) — K completions per prompt
    mean = rewards.mean(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
    std  = rewards.std(dim=-1, keepdim=True) + 1e-8
    return (rewards - mean) / std  # (B, K)
```

That short snippet, plus a clipped policy gradient, plus a verifiable
reward, plus weeks of compute, gets you a frontier reasoning model.

## What "thinking" actually looks like

R1's reasoning traces are remarkable to read. The model writes things like
*"Wait, let me reconsider..."*, *"This doesn't feel right, let me try a
different approach."*, *"Let me verify this calculation."* These behaviors
were never demonstrated to it; they emerged because they reduced loss under
the verifiable reward.

This is the deepest implication of R1-Zero: **reasoning is RL-discoverable
in pretrained LLMs**. The capability was latent in the base model. RL with
the right reward simply unlocked it.

<Callout variant="insight" title="The bitter lesson, again">
Sutton's bitter lesson: methods that scale with compute beat methods that
encode human priors. R1-Zero is the bitter lesson aimed at reasoning. We
spent a decade hand-engineering CoT prompts. RL discovered better ones from
scratch in weeks. Expect more of this.
</Callout>

## Where this is going

- **o3** (December 2024) demonstrated that the curve had not saturated:
  scaling up the same recipe by ~5× compute produced superhuman performance
  on ARC-AGI, FrontierMath, and competitive programming.
- **Distillation** of R1's reasoning into smaller models (R1-Distill-Qwen,
  R1-Distill-Llama) produced 7B-32B models that beat much larger base
  models on reasoning — without doing any RL themselves.
- **Domain transfer** is the open question. Verifiable rewards exist for
  math, code, and formal logic. They do not exist for "is this essay
  insightful?" Whether reasoning trained on verifiable domains transfers
  to non-verifiable ones is the research story of 2025–2026.

<Callout variant="warning" title="Don't conflate reasoning with thinking">
Long chain-of-thought is *correlated* with deliberation but is not
deliberation itself. The model can produce a 5,000-token "reasoning"
trace that is internally inconsistent, contains errors it does not catch,
or arrives at the right answer for the wrong reason. Use reasoning models
where their answers can be verified or audited. Don't trust their
introspection.
</Callout>

## Distillation: reasoning is teachable

A surprising R1 finding: once you have a strong reasoning teacher, you can
*distill* its reasoning behavior into a smaller model with plain SFT — no
RL needed. DeepSeek released R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, which beat much larger
non-reasoning models on math and code benchmarks despite being a routine
fine-tune on R1's traces.

This matters for two reasons. First, RL training is expensive and finicky;
SFT on traces is not. Second, it suggests reasoning is a relatively
compact, transferable behavior — once one model learns it, others can copy.
Expect more of the open-source ecosystem to converge on a pattern of
"frontier lab does the RL run, the rest of the field distills."
