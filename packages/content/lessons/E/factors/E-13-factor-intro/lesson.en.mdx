# Factor investing: an introduction

Why does one stock earn more than another over the long run? The single-stock answer is messy, but a powerful idea organizes it: returns are driven by a handful of common *factors* — measurable characteristics that have historically been rewarded. Value, size, momentum, quality, low-volatility. Factor investing builds portfolios that deliberately tilt toward these characteristics and collects the premium associated with each.

This lesson introduces the cross-section of returns — why stocks differ — and how a long-short factor portfolio isolates a single characteristic's payoff.

## The factor model

The workhorse equation decomposes each asset's return into factor exposures plus a residual:

$$
r_i = \alpha_i + \sum_{k} \beta_{ik} f_k + \epsilon_i
$$

Here $f_k$ is the return of factor $k$, $\beta_{ik}$ is asset $i$'s exposure (loading) to that factor, $\alpha_i$ is return unexplained by the factors, and $\epsilon_i$ is idiosyncratic noise. The Capital Asset Pricing Model is the one-factor special case ($f$ = the market). Factor investing says one factor is not enough: several characteristics independently command premiums.

## The classic factors

- **Value.** Cheap stocks (low price-to-book, low price-to-earnings) have historically outperformed expensive ones.
- **Size.** Smaller-cap stocks have, over long horizons, earned more than mega-caps (with higher risk).
- **Momentum.** Recent winners (past 3 to 12 months) tend to keep winning over the next few months.
- **Quality.** Profitable, low-debt, stable-earnings firms outperform junky ones.
- **Low-volatility.** Lower-risk stocks have delivered surprisingly strong risk-adjusted returns — a long-standing anomaly.

## Building a long-short factor portfolio

To isolate a factor's payoff you sort the universe by the characteristic, go long the top fraction and short the bottom, and hold the *spread*. For a quintile sort:

$$
f_k = \bar{r}_{\text{top quintile}} - \bar{r}_{\text{bottom quintile}}
$$

Long the cheapest 20% and short the most expensive 20%, and your portfolio return is the value premium itself — neutral to the market, exposed only to the value characteristic. This long-short construction is how academics measure factor returns and how factor funds harvest them.

SPY is pure *market* factor — one big beta with no tilt. A factor portfolio aims to add return streams that are partly independent of this line, so the blend is smoother than the market alone.

## The factor zoo problem

Once researchers learned that publishing a factor is rewarded, the literature exploded into hundreds of claimed factors — the 'factor zoo.' Most are statistical artifacts.

<Pitfall title="The factor zoo and p-hacking">
Hundreds of factors have been published, but many do not replicate out of sample. With thousands of candidate signals tested against the same few decades of data, some will clear a $t > 2$ bar purely by chance — classic p-hacking. When a 'factor' is re-tested on fresh data or a different market, a large fraction of the original premium vanishes. Demand an economic rationale, out-of-sample evidence, and a higher significance bar before trusting any factor beyond the few that have survived decades of scrutiny (value, momentum, size, quality, low-vol).
</Pitfall>

<KeyPoint title="Core idea">
Factor investing explains the cross-section of returns as exposure to common characteristics ($r_i = \alpha_i + \sum_k \beta_{ik} f_k + \epsilon_i$). Long-short sorts isolate each premium (value, size, momentum, quality, low-vol). Beware the factor zoo: most published factors are p-hacked and fail to replicate.
</KeyPoint>
