# Time-series momentum

Cross-sectional momentum asks "which assets are beating their peers?" Time-series momentum asks a simpler, more personal question of each asset: "has *this* asset been going up or down?" If its own trailing return is positive, you go long it; if negative, you go short it — independently, asset by asset. Moskowitz, Ooi, and Pedersen documented this effect across dozens of futures markets in 2012 and found it remarkably consistent. It is the quantitative backbone of trend-following CTAs.

The distinction from cross-sectional momentum matters: here there is no ranking and no peer comparison. Every asset is judged only against its own past.

## The signal: the sign of your own trailing return

For each asset, look back over a window (commonly 12 months) and take the sign of the return. The position for asset $i$ is:

$$
w_{i,t} = \mathrm{sign}\!\left(r_{i,\,t-k:t}\right)
$$

If the asset rose over the lookback, $w = +1$ (long); if it fell, $w = -1$ (short). Unlike cross-sectional momentum, **every asset can be long at once** (in a broad bull market) or **every asset short at once** (in a crash). The strategy has a net directional tilt that tracks the prevailing trend of its universe — which is exactly why it tends to make money in sustained market dislocations, up or down.

## Volatility scaling: the part that makes it work

A raw sign signal would let a wild asset dominate the book and a quiet one contribute nothing. Time-series momentum is almost always combined with **volatility scaling** — sizing each position inversely to its volatility so every asset contributes a similar risk budget:

$$
\text{position}_{i,t} = \frac{\sigma_{\text{target}}}{\sigma_{i,t}} \cdot w_{i,t}
$$

This does two jobs. It equalizes risk across a diverse universe (equities, bonds, commodities, FX), and it automatically cuts exposure when an asset's volatility spikes — which is precisely when trends tend to break. Vol scaling is not a cosmetic add-on; it is the mechanism that keeps the strategy's risk roughly constant through time.

## Turnover and crash risk

Because the sign can flip whenever an asset's trailing return crosses zero, and because positions are constantly re-scaled by volatility, the strategy **trades a lot**. High turnover means transaction costs matter enormously — a strategy that looks great on paper can be net-negative after realistic slippage and commissions (you will study these in Track D).

<Pitfall title="Same crash risk as momentum, amplified by turnover">
Time-series momentum shares cross-sectional momentum's negative skew: it grinds out steady gains, then suffers sharp losses at trend reversals, especially the V-shaped bottoms where a crashing market snaps back faster than the signal can flip. Worse, its high turnover means you pay costs constantly *and* you can get whipsawed by every false reversal. The defenses are non-negotiable: volatility scaling to keep risk constant and cut exposure as vol rises, plus disciplined cost modelling so you do not deploy a strategy whose paper edge evaporates into the bid-ask spread.
</Pitfall>

<KeyPoint title="Core idea">
Time-series momentum trades each asset on the sign of its own trailing return, volatility-scaled so every position carries similar risk. It captures sustained trends in any direction but inherits momentum's crash risk and adds heavy turnover — making vol scaling and cost control mandatory, not optional.
</KeyPoint>
