# Pairs trading

Trend following bets that moves continue. Pairs trading bets the opposite: that two related assets which have drifted apart will be pulled back together. It is the classic market-neutral mean-reversion strategy — you are long one name and short another, so the broad market's direction roughly cancels out and you profit from the *relationship* between the two, not from where the market goes.

The hard part is not the trade mechanics; it is deciding which pairs are genuinely tethered and recognizing when that tether has snapped.

## Cointegration: the right kind of "moving together"

Two stocks being correlated is not enough. Correlation measures whether returns move together day to day; it says nothing about whether the *prices* stay close. Two assets can be highly correlated and still wander arbitrarily far apart.

What you actually need is **cointegration**: a linear combination of the two price series that is stationary — it has a stable mean and reverts to it. If $y_t$ and $x_t$ are the two prices, you estimate a hedge ratio $\beta$ (typically by regressing $y$ on $x$) and form the **spread**:

$$
s_t = y_t - \beta x_t
$$

If the pair is cointegrated, $s_t$ oscillates around a constant mean rather than drifting. That stationary spread is the tradable object.

## The z-score: entry and exit on the spread

You do not trade the spread in raw price units; you standardize it. Using a rolling mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$ of the spread:

$$
z_t = \frac{s_t - \mu}{\sigma}
$$

A typical rule set:

- **Entry:** when $z_t > +2$, the spread is stretched high → short $y$, long $\beta$ units of $x$. When $z_t < -2$, do the reverse.
- **Exit:** close when $z_t$ returns toward $0$ (the spread has reverted).
- **Stop:** bail out if $|z_t|$ keeps widening past, say, $3$ or $4$ — a sign the relationship may have broken.

You are systematically betting that extreme dislocations in the spread are temporary.

## Half-life: how long until reversion?

Knowing a spread reverts is useless if it takes ten years. Model the spread as a mean-reverting (Ornstein–Uhlenbeck) process; the speed of reversion $\lambda$ gives a **half-life** — the expected time for a deviation to shrink by half:

$$
t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}
$$

The half-life tells you whether the pair is tradable on your horizon and helps size the rolling window for $\mu$ and $\sigma$. A half-life of a few days suits an intraday or daily trader; a half-life of a year is effectively a buy-and-hold bet on convergence.

## When the tether snaps

<Pitfall title="Cointegration is not a law of nature — it breaks">
The fatal risk in pairs trading is a structural break: the relationship that mean-reverted for years simply stops. A spread you are short at $z = +2$, expecting reversion, can instead diverge permanently. The textbook killer is a corporate event — one company in the pair gets acquired, changes its business, or goes bankrupt — and the prices that tracked each other for a decade detach forever. Your "mean-reverting" short now bleeds without bound. Always cap the loss with a stop on $|z|$, and re-test cointegration on a rolling basis rather than assuming yesterday's relationship still holds.
</Pitfall>

<KeyPoint title="Core idea">
Pairs trading is market-neutral mean reversion on a *cointegrated* spread $s_t = y_t - \beta x_t$, traded by z-score and timed by half-life. The edge depends entirely on the spread staying stationary — and your survival depends on a stop for when it doesn't.
</KeyPoint>
