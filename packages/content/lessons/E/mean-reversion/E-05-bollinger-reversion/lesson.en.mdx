# Bollinger mean reversion

Bollinger Bands draw a moving average with a volatility envelope above and below it. The mean-reversion strategy built on them is intuitive: when price stretches all the way to the upper band it is "too high" relative to recent behaviour and should snap back; when it touches the lower band it is "too low" and should bounce. You fade the extreme. You met the bands as an indicator in Track B (B-04/B-05); here we make a tradable rule out of them — and confront the one assumption that makes or breaks it.

## The band: a moving average plus volatility

The bands are built from an $n$-period moving average and the rolling standard deviation $\sigma$ of price, with a width multiplier $k$ (commonly 20 and 2):

$$
\text{Upper}_t = \mathrm{MA}_t + k\,\sigma_t, \qquad \text{Lower}_t = \mathrm{MA}_t - k\,\sigma_t
$$

Because the width scales with $\sigma$, the envelope breathes — it widens when the market is volatile and tightens when it is calm. A touch of the band is therefore not a fixed-percentage move; it is a move that is large *relative to current volatility*. That self-adjusting quality is what makes the bands appealing for mean reversion.

## The rule: fade the touch, exit at the middle

A long-only mean-reversion version:

- **Entry:** buy when price closes at or below the lower band (an oversold stretch).
- **Exit:** sell when price reverts to the middle band (the moving average).
- **Optional stop:** exit if price closes a further $k'\sigma$ below entry, capping the loss when the bounce never comes.

The logic is that in a sideways market, deviations of $2\sigma$ are statistically rare and tend to be corrected. You are harvesting the oscillation around a stable mean.

## Regime is everything

<Pitfall title="Band-touch reversion is destroyed by trends">
The reversion logic silently assumes the mean is *stable*. In a trending market it is not — and fading band touches becomes a way to stand in front of a freight train. The canonical cautionary tale: from 2003 to 2007 a buy-the-lower-band, sell-the-middle system on SPY looked spectacular, quietly buying every dip in a grinding bull market. Then 2008 arrived. Price did not touch the lower band and bounce; it slammed through the lower band and kept falling for months, while the strategy bought again and again into a collapse. Mean reversion without a regime filter does not have a bad year — it blows up. You must gate the strategy: only fade touches when a trend filter (e.g. a flat 200-day average, or low ADX) says the market is ranging, and stand aside when it says the market is trending.
</Pitfall>

## Making it robust

Practical defenses against the trend trap:

- **Regime filter:** require the longer-term trend to be flat before taking reversion trades.
- **Hard stop:** never trade reversion without a stop; an "oversold" market can get far more oversold.
- **Asymmetric sizing:** size for the failure case, not the typical case, because the rare trend-blowup loss dwarfs the many small reversion wins.

<KeyPoint title="Core idea">
Bollinger reversion fades $2\sigma$ band touches back to the mean. It works beautifully in range regimes and detonates in trends. The strategy is only as good as the regime filter that decides when to switch it off.
</KeyPoint>
