# Breakout strategies

A breakout strategy waits for price to escape a well-defined range and bets that the escape marks the start of a new move. Where the Donchian system (E-02) defined the range purely by an N-bar high, the broader breakout family lets you draw the level any way that captures where supply and demand have been balanced: a horizontal support or resistance line, the top of a multi-week consolidation, a chart-pattern neckline. The trade is the same in spirit — buy strength as it clears the ceiling, sell weakness as it breaks the floor.

The whole game is separating a *real* breakout from a *false* one.

## Defining the level

A breakout level is a price the market has repeatedly failed to cross. The more times price has tested a level and bounced, the more orders (stops, limits) cluster just beyond it — and the more meaningful a clean break becomes. Common ways to define the level:

- **Horizontal resistance/support:** the high (or low) of a recent congestion zone.
- **Range boundary:** the top or bottom of a sideways channel the market has traded inside.
- **Pattern level:** the neckline of a base, the top of a triangle, a prior swing high.

The entry triggers when price moves decisively beyond the level; the initial stop sits just back inside the range, so a failed breakout is cut quickly.

## Volume confirmation

Price alone is a weak breakout signal; **volume is the corroborating witness**. A genuine breakout — driven by real demand overwhelming the supply parked at the level — usually comes on a surge in volume. A breakout on thin volume is suspect: it may just be a few orders nudging price across a quiet level with no conviction behind it. A simple confirmation filter compares breakout-bar volume to its recent average:

$$
\text{confirmed} = \mathbf{1}\!\left[\,P_t > R \;\land\; V_t > m \cdot \overline{V}_{n}\,\right]
$$

Here $R$ is the resistance level, $\overline{V}_n$ is the average volume over the prior $n$ bars, and $m$ (e.g. 1.5–2) is the surge multiple. Both conditions must hold: a close beyond the level *and* a volume expansion.

## The fakeout problem

<Pitfall title="False breakouts ('fakeouts') are the default failure mode">
The single most common way a breakout trade dies is the fakeout: price pokes just beyond the level, triggers a wave of breakout buyers (and stop orders), then immediately reverses back into the range — often a deliberate flush that hunts the stops resting beyond the level. Two defenses, used together: (1) **demand confirmation** — require a *close* beyond the level, not just an intraday spike, plus the volume surge above; intrabar pokes are cheap to fake, closes are not. (2) **Size for the failure case** — assume a meaningful fraction of your breakouts will fail, place a tight stop just inside the range, and size the position so that a fakeout is a small, survivable loss rather than a catastrophe. You cannot eliminate fakeouts; you can make them cheap.
</Pitfall>

<KeyPoint title="Core idea">
A breakout strategy buys a decisive move beyond a tested level and bets on continuation. Require a close beyond the level plus a volume surge to filter out fakeouts, and size every entry for the failure case — because false breakouts, not real ones, are the norm.
</KeyPoint>
