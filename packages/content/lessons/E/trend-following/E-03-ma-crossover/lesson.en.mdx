# Moving-average crossover

The moving-average crossover is the "hello world" of trend following: a fast moving average crosses above a slow one and you go long; it crosses back below and you go flat or short. It is the first systematic strategy almost every quant codes, because the logic is trivial and the data requirement is just a price series. You met the SMA and EMA as indicators in Track B (B-01/B-02); here we turn two of them into a complete entry/exit rule.

Simple does not mean good. Understanding *why* this strategy works — and when it fails badly — is more valuable than the rule itself.

## The rule: sign of the gap

Pick a fast lookback $f$ and a slow lookback $s$ with $f < s$ (the famous pair is 50 and 200 days). Compute both moving averages and trade the sign of their difference:

$$
\text{signal}_t = \mathrm{sign}\!\left(\mathrm{MA}^{f}_t - \mathrm{MA}^{s}_t\right)
$$

When $\text{signal}_t = +1$ you are long; when $-1$ you are flat or short. The crossing of the 50-day up through the 200-day is the celebrated **golden cross**; the 50-day falling below the 200-day is the **death cross**. These names get a lot of press, but mechanically they are nothing more than the moment the sign above flips.

## Why it captures trends

A fast average reflects recent prices; a slow average reflects the longer backdrop. When the fast rises above the slow, recent prices are systematically higher than the longer-run level — the definition of an uptrend in progress. The crossover is a coarse but honest trend detector: it commits you to the direction the market has actually been moving, with no forecasting required.

<Chart symbol="SPY" height={360} intervalLabel="D" />

On the SPY chart, picture a 50- and 200-day line. In the long bull runs the fast line sits cleanly above the slow line and you hold a winning long the whole way. The strategy's entire return comes from a handful of episodes like these.

## The lag problem

Every moving average is a backward-looking average, so a crossover always confirms a trend *after* it has begun and signals its end *after* it has turned. You buy late and sell late. In a strong, sustained trend this lag costs you only the first and last slices of the move — a price worth paying. The trouble is what happens between trends.

<Pitfall title="Choppy markets generate losing whipsaws">
In a sideways, range-bound market the fast and slow averages cross back and forth repeatedly, generating a string of buy-high, sell-low whipsaw trades that each lose a little. The strategy can be net unprofitable for years and still be working as designed — because most of the long-run return comes from a few big trends, and the choppy periods between them are a tax you pay to stay positioned for the next one. If you cannot stomach long flat-to-negative stretches, you will abandon the strategy right before the trend that would have paid for it all.
</Pitfall>

<KeyPoint title="Core idea">
A crossover trades the sign of (fast MA − slow MA). It lags by construction, loses small amounts to whipsaws in ranges, and earns its keep from a few large trends. The skill is surviving the boredom between them.
</KeyPoint>
