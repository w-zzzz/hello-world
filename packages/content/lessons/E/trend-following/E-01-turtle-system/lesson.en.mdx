# The Turtle trading system

In 1983, commodity traders Richard Dennis and William Eckhardt made a bet: could trading be taught? They recruited a group of novices — the Turtles — handed them a fully mechanical rulebook, and let them trade real money. Many became hugely profitable. The Turtle system is the canonical worked example of a complete trend-following strategy: it specifies entry, exit, position sizing, and risk in one coherent package.

You already met the Donchian channel as an indicator in Track B. This lesson uses it as the engine of a full system. The point is not the channel itself — it is how entry, sizing, pyramiding, and stops fit together into something you could actually trade.

## Entry and exit: the Donchian breakout core

The classic Turtle entry is a **20-day Donchian breakout**: go long when price makes a new 20-day high, go short when it makes a new 20-day low. You are not predicting the trend — you are reacting to evidence that one has begun.

The exit is a shorter, opposite channel: close a long when price makes a new **10-day low**, close a short on a new 10-day high. The asymmetry is deliberate. A slow 20-day entry filters out small wiggles; a fast 10-day exit gives back less of an established trend when it turns.

## Sizing with N: volatility as the ruler

The Turtles sized positions not in dollars or shares first, but in **volatility units**. They defined `N` as the 20-day exponential average of the True Range — essentially the ATR you saw in Track B. `N` answers one question: how much does this market move in a normal day?

Position size comes from equalizing risk across markets. With account equity $E$ and a fixed risk fraction (the Turtles used 1% per unit), the number of contracts is:

$$
\text{Unit} = \frac{0.01 \times E}{N \times \text{DollarsPerPoint}}
$$

A quiet market gets a larger position; a wild market gets a smaller one. Each position therefore risks roughly the same dollar amount per `N` of adverse move, regardless of the instrument.

## Pyramiding: adding to winners

The Turtles did not enter their full size at once. They added **units** as the trade moved in their favor — typically one extra unit for every $\tfrac{1}{2}N$ of further progress, up to a maximum of four units. This is pyramiding: you scale into strength, so your biggest position rides the trades that turn into the rare, enormous trends that pay for everything else.

The flip side is the **2N stop**. Each unit carries a stop placed $2N$ away from its entry. As you add units, you raise the stop on the whole stack, so a reversal closes the entire position near break-even. Risk is capped; upside is left open.

## Why a published edge decays

<Pitfall title="A published system is a crowded system">
The original Turtle edge was real — and then it was published, taught, and coded into thousands of retail systems. As more capital chases the same 20-day breakouts, breakouts get front-run, whipsaws multiply, and the edge thins. The lesson generalizes: any purely mechanical rule, once widely known, competes its own returns away. Treat the Turtle rules as a template for *structure*, not as a money machine to run verbatim.
</Pitfall>

<KeyPoint title="What makes it a system">
The Turtle system is worth studying because it is complete: entry (20-day breakout), exit (10-day reverse), sizing (risk a fixed fraction per N), pyramiding (add to winners), and a hard stop (2N). A signal is not a strategy until all five are specified.
</KeyPoint>
