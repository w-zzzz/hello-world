# Commissions and fees: the toll you pay on every trade

Slippage is the price the market charges you; commissions are the price your broker and the exchange charge you. They are smaller and more predictable, but they are perfectly real, and for high-frequency, small-edge strategies they are often the difference between profit and ruin. This lesson teaches you the common fee models and how to compute the edge a strategy needs just to break even.

## Three ways brokers bill you

Most cost schedules reduce to one of three shapes:

- **Per-share**: e.g. \$0.005 per share. Cheap for high-priced stocks, expensive for penny stocks where you trade many shares per dollar.
- **Per-trade (flat)**: e.g. \$1 per order regardless of size. Brutal for small accounts; negligible for large blocks.
- **Basis points (bps) of notional**: e.g. 5 bps of trade value. The standard for institutions and most crypto venues; scales naturally with position size.

On top of these sit exchange fees, regulatory fees (SEC, FINRA in the US), and sometimes a maker-taker rebate that pays you for adding liquidity and charges you for taking it.

## Round-trip cost

A position has to be opened and closed, so the cost that matters is the **round-trip**: entry fee plus exit fee. If a one-way commission is $c$ (as a fraction of notional), the round-trip is

$$
C_{\text{round-trip}} = c_{\text{in}} + c_{\text{out}} \approx 2c
$$

This is the number you compare your edge against — not the one-way fee. Beginners quote the one-way cost and quietly halve their true hurdle.

## The break-even edge

For a strategy to make money, the average gross profit per trade must exceed the round-trip cost. Let $e$ be your expected gross edge per trade and $C$ the round-trip cost, both in the same units (say, bps of notional). You need

$$
e > C, \quad \text{and for a margin of safety} \quad e \ge 2C
$$

The factor of two is a practical rule: estimates of edge are noisy and optimistic, while costs are firm. If your modeled edge is only barely above costs, the live result will almost certainly land below them.

## A worked example

Say a mean-reversion strategy shows a 7 bps average gross profit per trade in backtest. Commissions are 1.5 bps one-way, so 3 bps round-trip; add 3 bps of modeled slippage round-trip and total cost is 6 bps. Net edge: 7 − 6 = 1 bps. Apply the $e \ge 2C$ safety rule — you would want 12 bps gross to feel comfortable, and you have 7. This strategy is **net-negative once realistic costs and noise are included**, even though the gross backtest looked positive.

<Pitfall title="A tiny per-trade edge dies even at low commissions">
It is tempting to wave away a 2 bps commission as negligible. But if your strategy's gross edge is only 5 bps per trade, that 2 bps one-way (4 bps round-trip) eats most of it — and that is before slippage. Always compute the required edge as at least twice the all-in cost. A high-turnover strategy with a thin per-trade edge is the classic way a positive backtest becomes a negative track record.
</Pitfall>

<KeyPoint title="Compare gross edge to round-trip, not one-way">
Your hurdle is the round-trip cost (entry + exit), not the one-way commission. Demand a gross edge of at least 2x that hurdle to leave room for noisy edge estimates and unmodeled costs.
</KeyPoint>
