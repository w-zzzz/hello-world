# Log Returns and Why They Add

Last lesson we saw that simple returns must be multiplied to compound them, which is awkward in both code and algebra. Log returns use a clever transform that turns multiplication into addition, and that is exactly why they are everywhere in quant work. This lesson covers how to compute a log return, why log returns add across time, how they relate to simple returns, and one boundary people constantly confuse: log returns add across time but they do **not** add across assets.

## Definition

A log return is the natural log of the price ratio:

$$
\ell_t = \ln\!\left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\right) = \ln P_t - \ln P_{t-1}
$$

It is also called the continuously-compounded return. Its relationship to the simple return is $\ell_t = \ln(1 + r_t)$, and inversely $r_t = e^{\ell_t} - 1$.

## The key property: additive across time

Take the log of the product of simple growth factors and the product becomes a sum:

$$
\sum_{t=1}^{T} \ell_t = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \ln\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}} = \ln\frac{P_T}{P_0}
$$

The intermediate terms telescope away, leaving only the endpoints. So **summing multi-period log returns gives the total log return over the whole span**. That makes many tasks elegant: N-day cumulative returns, time-series regressions, and volatility estimates are all cleaner with log returns.

<KeyPoint title="Log returns add across time">$\sum_t \ell_t = \ln(P_T/P_0)$. Add up the daily log returns and you have the whole-period log return, no multiplication needed. This is the single most useful property of log returns.</KeyPoint>

## For small returns, $\ell \approx r$

When returns are small, a Taylor expansion gives $\ln(1+r) \approx r - r^2/2 + \cdots$, so:

$$
\ell_t \approx r_t \quad (\text{for small } r_t)
$$

Intraday and daily returns are usually a fraction of a percent, where log and simple returns are nearly identical. For $r = 1\%$, $\ell = \ln(1.01) \approx 0.995\%$, a negligible gap. But the gap grows with size: at $r = 50\%$, $\ell = \ln(1.5) \approx 40.5\%$.

## The boundary: do not add across assets

This is the most common beginner trap. Log returns add across the **time** dimension but not across the **asset** dimension. A portfolio's one-period return is the weighted sum of its components' **simple** returns:

$$
r_{p,t} = \sum_{i} w_i \, r_{i,t}
$$

whereas $\sum_i w_i \ell_{i,t} \neq \ell_{p,t}$, because the log function is not linear, so it does not commute with a weighted sum. In short: to compound one asset over many periods, sum log returns; to combine many assets in one period, go back to weighting simple returns.

<Pitfall title="Log returns cannot be weighted across assets">Weighting each stock's log return and calling the result the portfolio return is wrong. The portfolio return is the weighted sum of component **simple** returns. The correct flow: convert log returns back to simple returns $r_i = e^{\ell_i}-1$, weight them into a portfolio simple return, then convert back to log if needed. Confusing the two dimensions biases your portfolio-level backtest systematically.</Pitfall>

## Summary

Log returns win on being additive across time and friendly to statistical modeling; their red line is that they are not additive across assets. Remember: logs for the time dimension, simple returns for the asset dimension.
