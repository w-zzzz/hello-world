# Simple Returns: Arithmetic and Cumulative

The first step in any backtest is translating a price series into a return series. Prices alone are hard to compare: a move from \$10 to \$11 and a move from \$100 to \$101 are both up one dollar, but they mean very different things to a holder. Returns normalize price changes into percentages so you can compare across prices and across assets. This lesson covers the most basic building block, the simple return: how to compute one period, how to chain many periods, and the averaging trap nearly every beginner falls into.

## Single-period simple return

From $t-1$ to $t$, with price moving from $P_{t-1}$ to $P_t$, the simple return is:

$$
r_t = \frac{P_t - P_{t-1}}{P_{t-1}} = \frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}} - 1
$$

If yesterday closed at \$100 and today closed at \$102, then $r_t = 0.02$, or +2%. A drop to \$98 gives $r_t = -0.02$, or −2%. Simple returns are intuitive and they aggregate cleanly across a portfolio: a portfolio's return in one period is the weighted average of its components' simple returns. Hold onto that property; it matters when we contrast it with log returns next lesson.

## Cumulative returns: multiply, do not add

Hold across several periods and the total return is **not** the sum of each period's return. You multiply the growth factors $(1+r_t)$:

$$
1 + R = \prod_{t=1}^{T} (1 + r_t)
$$

Here $R$ is the cumulative return from start to finish. Say day one is +10% and day two is −10%:

$$
1 + R = (1.10)(0.90) = 0.99
$$

So $R = -1\%$. Up 10% then down 10% does not return you to par; you are down 1%. This is the asymmetry of compounding: after a loss you need a larger gain to recover. Lose 50% and you need a 100% gain just to get back to even.

<KeyPoint title="Cumulative return compounds">Multiply each $(1+r_t)$ to get the total growth factor, then subtract one for the cumulative return. Adding returns is only an approximation that breaks down once periods pile up and volatility rises.</KeyPoint>

The product lands exactly on end price over start price, which is just the cumulative formula rewritten: $1+R = P_T/P_0$.

## Arithmetic vs geometric mean

Many people use an average daily return to eyeball strategy performance, and that hides a classic trap. The arithmetic mean sums the returns and divides by the count. But what actually drives your ending balance is the **geometric mean**:

$$
\bar{r}_{\text{geo}} = \left( \prod_{t=1}^{T}(1+r_t) \right)^{1/T} - 1
$$

Whenever returns vary, the arithmetic mean is **always greater than or equal to** the geometric mean, with the gap roughly half the variance. Using the arithmetic mean to project compounded growth systematically overstates it, and the overstatement grows with volatility.

<Pitfall title="Averaging simple returns overstates compounded growth">A fund up 50% one year and down 50% the next has an arithmetic mean of 0%, which sounds like a wash. But the geometric mean is $\sqrt{1.5 \times 0.5}-1 \approx -13.4\%$; over the two years you actually lost 25%. When reporting performance or projecting long-run growth, use the geometric mean (the compound annual growth rate), not the flattering arithmetic figure.</Pitfall>
