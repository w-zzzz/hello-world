# Maximum Drawdown

Returns tell you how much you made; drawdown tells you how painful the worst stretch was along the way. For real traders, maximum drawdown (MDD) often decides whether you can stick with a strategy more than total return does. An equity curve that compounds at 20% a year but draws down 60% in the middle is one almost nobody holds through the bottom. This lesson defines drawdown, shows how to compute the maximum, and explains a frequently overlooked but lethal property: MDD is path-dependent and sample-length-dependent.

## What is drawdown

Drawdown measures how far the current equity sits below its prior peak. Let $P_t$ be equity (or price) at time $t$, and let the running peak through $t$ be $\max_{s\le t} P_s$. The drawdown at $t$ is:

$$
\mathrm{DD}_t = \frac{P_t - \max_{s\le t} P_s}{\max_{s\le t} P_s}
$$

Drawdown is always $\le 0$: it is zero whenever equity makes a new high and turns negative once it falls below the peak.

## Maximum drawdown is the deepest one

Maximum drawdown is the minimum (deepest trough) of the drawdown series:

$$
\mathrm{MDD} = \min_{t} \mathrm{DD}_t = \min_{t}\frac{P_t - \max_{s\le t}P_s}{\max_{s\le t}P_s}
$$

Worked example: equity runs 100 → 120 → 90 → 110. Point by point: at 100 the peak is 100, DD=0; at 120 a new high, DD=0; at 90 the peak is still 120, DD=(90−120)/120=−25%; at 110 the peak is 120, DD=(110−120)/120≈−8.3%. The deepest is −25%, so MDD = −25%. Note the peak is the highest so far and never gets revised down just because price later fell.

<KeyPoint title="MDD is the deepest peak-to-trough loss">Track the running peak, compute each point's loss relative to that peak, and take the most negative one. It answers: if you bought at the worst possible high, how far underwater would you have gone?</KeyPoint>

A good equity curve is not one without drawdowns; it is one whose drawdowns are shallow and recover quickly. Of two strategies with the same total return, the one with shallower, faster-recovering drawdowns is more livable and more sustainable in real trading.

## The lethal property: path and sample-length dependence

MDD is **path-dependent**: it depends on the specific route prices took, not just the start and end. With the same total return, up-then-down and down-then-up produce completely different MDDs.

More insidious is **sample-length dependence**: the longer the history you look at, the deeper the worst drawdown almost always becomes. Extend a backtest window from 3 years to 10 and the MDD typically worsens, not because the strategy degraded but because a longer window is more likely to contain a major bear market.

<Pitfall title="Never compare MDD across unequal windows">MDD worsens monotonically with sample length: a longer window is more likely to sample the extreme low. Comparing one strategy's 5-year MDD of −18% against another's 20-year MDD of −35% is unfair; the second was simply observed for longer. Always align the same window when comparing MDDs, or supplement with a window-insensitive measure like annualized volatility. Likewise, MDD only describes the worst path that already happened; it does not bound how deep the future can go.</Pitfall>
