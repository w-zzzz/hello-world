# Detecting market regimes

A strategy that prints money in a calm trending tape can lose every penny back in a choppy, volatile one. The same RSI cross, the same moving-average rule — different *regime*, different outcome. This lesson is about how to tell which world you are in, and how to use that knowledge as a filter rather than a crystal ball.

By the end you will be able to define what a regime is, name the three taxonomies practitioners actually use, and explain why regime indicators are themselves lagging.

## What is a regime?

A **regime** is a window of relatively stable market behavior — stable enough that statistical properties (drift, volatility, autocorrelation) look roughly constant. Regimes don't have crisp edges. They blur into one another, and you only really know a regime ended after it has ended. The point is not to predict the next regime; it is to recognize the current one well enough to avoid running a mean-reverting bot inside a runaway trend.

## Taxonomy 1: trend vs range

The most useful split for systematic traders is **trending vs ranging**. ADX is the classical measurement.

- **ADX > 25** is conventionally read as a trending regime.
- **ADX < 20** is read as a ranging regime.
- The zone between is ambiguous — treat it as "unknown" rather than forcing a label.

A model-free alternative is realized-vol of returns combined with autocorrelation: trends have positive return autocorrelation at short lags; ranges have negative.

## Taxonomy 2: high vs low volatility

Volatility regimes change strategy expectations. A breakout system needs vol; a carry system hates it. The simplest measure is annualized realized volatility over the last $N$ bars:

$$
\mathrm{RV}_t \;=\; \sqrt{\,\frac{252}{N}\sum_{i=t-N+1}^{t} r_i^{\,2}\,}
$$

A rolling median of $\mathrm{RV}$ over a long window (e.g. one year) is your threshold. Above median is "high vol"; below median is "low vol". Crude — and very hard to beat without overfitting.

## Taxonomy 3: risk-on vs risk-off

Macro practitioners watch cross-asset relationships. When stocks rally and bonds sell off together, capital is moving out of safety — risk-on. When the SPY/TLT ratio compresses, dollar firms up against EM, and credit spreads widen, capital is hiding — risk-off. These aren't precise rules; they are dashboards. You combine them into a single discretionary or rules-based "risk gauge".

## Probabilistic regime models

A more rigorous approach is a **Hidden Markov Model (HMM)** with, say, two latent states $\{R_1, R_2\}$ and observed returns. The model learns transition probabilities $P(R_{t+1} \mid R_t)$ and emits a posterior $P(R_t \mid \text{data}_{\le t})$. You act when the posterior of one state is high.

HMMs are mathematically elegant and operationally fragile. They overfit short samples, they reassign labels between fits, and the "regimes" they find don't always match anything tradable. Use them as a research tool, not a production switch — at least until you've walked them forward across multiple years.

## How to use regime: as a filter, not a forecast

The temptation is to treat regime as a predictor: "we're in low-vol mean reversion, so I'll go long mean reversion now." That confuses *current* regime with *future* regime. A safer framing:

- Every strategy has a **preferred regime**. Mean reversion likes range + low vol. Trend following likes momentum + high vol.
- A regime filter **keeps you out of obviously wrong regimes** for your strategy. It does not tell you the right regime is coming.
- When two strategies with opposite regime preferences are combined honestly, you get a portfolio whose total exposure is regulated by the filter.

<Pitfall title="Regime indicators are themselves lagging">
By the time ADX confirms a trend, you have already paid the cost of the entire range that preceded it — and the trend may be near exhaustion. Treat regime as a filter that removes the worst environments, not as a signal that calls the next one. If your strategy needs a regime forecast to be profitable, it is probably not robust.
</Pitfall>

<KeyPoint title="The honest use of regime">
Don't ask "what regime is next?" Ask "is the current regime hostile to my strategy?" A regime filter shrinks your exposure when the answer is yes.
</KeyPoint>
