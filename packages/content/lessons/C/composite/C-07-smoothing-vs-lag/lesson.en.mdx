# Smoothing vs lag — a fundamental trade-off

Every smoothing knob you turn down to reduce noise quietly turns up another knob called lag. You cannot escape this. It is the same reason a thermostat with heavy averaging is calm but slow, while one with no averaging is jumpy but fast. This lesson is about treating that trade-off honestly instead of pretending some magic parameter solves it.

## Why smoothing always lags

A moving average is, by construction, an average over past values. Information from bar $t$ shows up in a window that ends at bar $t$, so the centre of the window sits at $t - n/2$. That offset *is* the lag.

For an exponentially weighted moving average with smoothing factor $\alpha = 2 / (n + 1)$, the effective lag is approximately:

$$
\text{lag}_{\text{EWMA}} \;\approx\; \frac{n - 1}{2}
$$

So a 20-period EMA carries roughly 9–10 bars of lag. Want less lag? Lower $n$. But the variance of the smoothed series is roughly $\sigma^2 / n_{\text{eff}}$ where $n_{\text{eff}} \approx (2 - \alpha)/\alpha$, so dropping $n$ to 5 doubles the noise. Pick your poison.

## Three options for managing lag

### 1. Lower the window

The simplest move. Less smoothing, less lag, more false signals. Useful when your downstream filter (a confluence rule, a regime gate) can tolerate the extra noise.

### 2. Use a smarter filter

Filters like Kalman smoothers, Hodrick-Prescott, or Savitzky-Golay can, in theory, achieve lower lag at the same noise level — but only if their assumed model matches reality. They introduce **model risk**: when the market regime changes, the assumed dynamics no longer hold and the "smart" filter can lag worse than a dumb EMA. Sophistication is not always robustness.

### 3. Lead/lag substitutes

Combine a fast oscillator (e.g. RSI(7)) with a slow trend filter (e.g. SMA(200)). The oscillator gives near-zero lag on local extremes; the trend filter contributes near-zero noise on the broad direction. Together they approximate a signal that is both responsive and steady — at the cost of two parameters instead of one.

The hard lesson: on the same dataset, **no single period is best**. The best period in a trending year is too slow in a choppy year, and vice versa.

## The temptation — and why it fails

Every new quant goes through a phase of grid-searching the smoothing parameter to find "the optimum." It always exists in-sample. It rarely survives out-of-sample. The reason is structural: the optimum is a function of the regime, and the regime changes. A parameter that looks brilliant on 2017–2019 looks foolish on 2020 and again on 2022.

<Pitfall title="The "perfect smoothing parameter" is a mirage">
There is no period that is robustly optimal across regimes. Searching for one — and reporting the in-sample winner — is one of the most reliable ways to deceive yourself with a backtest. Robust strategies are deliberately **un-clever** about smoothing: round numbers, picked a priori, justified on signal-processing grounds, not on backtest Sharpe.
</Pitfall>

## A practical posture

Pick smoothing windows from a small, defensible set (5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200) before you backtest. Document **why** you chose each. Re-validate that they still make sense on walk-forward windows, but resist the urge to grid-search.

If you find yourself needing aggressive smoothing to make a signal work, ask whether the underlying signal is real. Heavy smoothing can hide a weak edge — and lag it into uselessness.

<KeyPoint title="Two facts to keep together">
1. Every bar of smoothing is a bar of lag.
2. The optimal smoothing window changes with the regime, so optimizing it once and freezing it overfits the regime you measured in.
</KeyPoint>
