# Forming a testable hypothesis

Every strategy that survives contact with live markets started as a sentence you could be wrong about. Track H is about shipping a strategy, and the first thing you ship is not code — it is a claim. This lesson teaches you to write a hypothesis that is specific enough to test, falsifiable enough to be honest, and grounded in a reason the edge should exist at all.

The bar is higher than it sounds. Most strategy ideas, written down plainly, turn out to be either untestable, unfalsifiable, or pure pattern-chasing. Get the hypothesis right and the rest of the track — universe, prototype, costs, walk-forward — becomes a disciplined series of yes/no questions.

## What makes a hypothesis testable

A testable hypothesis names four things: the **market**, the **signal**, the **direction and horizon**, and the **conditions** under which it should work. Compare these two:

- Bad: 'RSI below 30 makes money.'
- Good: 'In large-cap US equities, a close with RSI(14) below 30 predicts a positive 5-day forward return, because forced sellers create a temporary liquidity discount.'

The second can be checked, dated, and refuted. The first cannot — it has no horizon, no universe, no measurable claim.

<KeyPoint title="The shape of a hypothesis">A usable hypothesis fits the template: in [universe], [signal] predicts [direction] over [horizon], because [economic reason], and should be stronger when [condition].</KeyPoint>

## Why does this edge exist — and persist?

This is the question that separates research from data-mining. Markets are competitive: if an edge is real and obvious, capital floods in and arbitrages it away. So you must answer two things.

First, **why does the edge exist?** Usually it is a structural reason — someone is forced to trade for non-price reasons (margin calls, index rebalances, redemptions, risk limits), or someone is paid to bear a risk others avoid.

Second, **why does it persist?** Maybe the opportunity is too small for large funds, or it lives in a constrained corner of the market, or it pays for a genuine risk that does not disappear.

A worked example: oversold bounces in large caps. The hypothesis is that forced sellers under margin calls create a temporary liquidity premium. The prediction follows naturally — the effect should be **stronger in high-volatility regimes** (more forced selling) and should **decay as liquidity providers arrive**.

## Turning the claim into a number

A hypothesis you can test is one you can write as an expectation. Let $r_{t+h}$ be the forward return over horizon $h$ and let $S_t$ be your signal condition. The claim is simply:

$$
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\, r_{t+h} \mid S_t = 1 \,\right] > \mathbb{E}\!\left[\, r_{t+h} \mid S_t = 0 \,\right]
$$

Once it is in this form, the prototype writes itself: compute the conditional means, and check whether the difference is large enough to matter and stable enough to trust. Before you touch the [editor](/en/editor), you should already know which number would confirm the claim and which would refute it.

## A regime to keep in mind

Look at SPY across a calm stretch and then a volatile one. Your oversold-bounce hypothesis predicts the signal behaves differently in each — strong bounces when volatility is high, muted ones when it is low. Holding that prediction in mind before you measure is what makes the test honest.

## The cardinal sin: starting from the data

<Pitfall title="Reverse-engineering noise">
If you scan the data first, find a pattern, and then write the hypothesis to fit it, you have not discovered an edge — you have described historical noise. With enough indicators and thresholds, something always looks profitable in-sample. Write the hypothesis and its economic rationale first, then test it once. A pattern with no prior reason to exist is the textbook setup for an overfit strategy that dies the moment you trade it.
</Pitfall>
