# Equal weighting (1/N)

Once you have picked the assets you want to hold, you face a second decision that matters at least as much: how much of each? The simplest possible answer is to split your capital evenly. With $N$ assets, every position gets the same dollar weight. It sounds almost too naive to take seriously — yet a famous study by DeMiguel, Garlappi, and Uppal (2009) found that across many datasets, no fancier optimizer reliably beat it out of sample.

This lesson is the foundation of Track G. We will define the 1/N rule, see why it is so hard to beat, and expose the one assumption it quietly makes that can hurt you.

## The rule

Equal weighting assigns each of $N$ assets the same fraction of capital:

$$
w_i = \frac{1}{N}, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i = 1
$$

If you hold 5 names, each gets 20% of the book. There is nothing to estimate — no expected returns, no covariance matrix, no parameters to fit. That is precisely its strength.

## Why it is hard to beat

Every more sophisticated method needs *inputs*: expected returns, volatilities, correlations. Those inputs must be estimated from finite, noisy history, and the estimates carry error. A clever optimizer fed bad estimates produces confident-looking but fragile weights.

The 1/N rule estimates nothing, so it has no estimation error to amplify. When the edge from optimizing is smaller than the noise in your inputs — which is common with short samples and many assets — the parameter-free baseline wins. Treat 1/N as the bar every other method in this track must clear.

## A worked example

Suppose you hold 4 ETFs. Equal weighting puts 25% in each:

- Asset A: 25%
- Asset B: 25%
- Asset C: 25%

## The hidden catch: equal dollars is not equal risk

Here is the assumption 1/N makes silently: that splitting *dollars* evenly is a sensible way to split *risk*. It usually is not.

Suppose three assets sit at 25% each, but one of them is twice as volatile as the others. That single name can contribute far more than a quarter of the portfolio's variance. Your dollar allocation looks balanced while your *risk* allocation is dominated by one position. The next two lessons (inverse-vol and risk parity) exist to fix exactly this gap.

<Pitfall title="1/N ignores risk">
An equal-dollar weight is not an equal-risk weight. If one holding is much more volatile than the others, it can dominate total portfolio variance even at the same dollar weight. You may *think* you are diversified while one name drives most of your swings.
</Pitfall>

<KeyPoint title="The baseline to beat">
1/N estimates no parameters, so it carries no estimation error. That makes it a startlingly strong benchmark — but it allocates dollars, not risk, so a volatile name can quietly dominate the book.
</KeyPoint>
