# Track G review

You have now walked the full ladder of portfolio construction, from the parameter-free 1/N rule all the way up to Black-Litterman. This closing lesson ties the track together with one organizing question that runs through every method: *how much do you trust your estimates?* The more you trust them, the more sophisticated a method you can justify — and the more you can hurt yourself if that trust is misplaced.

## The ladder of trust

Each method we studied uses more inputs than the one before, and each new input must be estimated from noisy data:

- **1/N (G-01)** — trusts nothing. No estimates at all; equal dollars.
- **Inverse-vol (G-02)** — trusts volatilities only.
- **Risk parity (G-03)** — trusts the full covariance matrix.
- **MVO (G-04)** — trusts covariance *and* expected returns. The most demanding, and the most fragile.
- **Ledoit-Wolf (G-05)** — makes the covariance estimate reliable enough to use.
- **Black-Litterman (G-06)** — makes the expected-return estimate reliable enough to use.

Reading top to bottom, you are climbing a ladder of trust in your inputs. Each rung needs more, and more *reliable*, information than the last.

## Estimation error is the enemy

The thread connecting the whole track is estimation error. Volatilities are the easiest to estimate well; correlations are harder; expected returns are hardest of all. That ordering explains the whole ladder. A method's robustness can be roughly captured as:

$$
\text{usable edge} = \text{theoretical edge} - \text{estimation error amplified by the method}
$$

MVO has the highest theoretical edge but amplifies error the most, which is why it needs Ledoit-Wolf and Black-Litterman as crutches. 1/N has no theoretical edge but amplifies nothing — so its *usable* edge is often competitive.

## Rebalancing ties it off

Whatever weights you choose, the rebalancing module (G-07 to G-09) governs how you maintain them: frequency trades cost against drift, turnover-aware optimization bakes cost into the objective, and the workshop made the cost-versus-risk trade-off concrete. Construction sets the target; rebalancing keeps you near it without bleeding to costs.

## When to use what

Use 1/N or risk parity when your estimates are weak or your universe is large and unstable. Reach for MVO with shrinkage and Black-Litterman only when you genuinely have reliable views and a clean covariance estimate. Match the method's appetite for inputs to the quality of inputs you actually have.

<Pitfall title="Complexity is not edge">
A more sophisticated optimizer is not automatically better. Because MVO amplifies estimation error, an over-fit MVO routinely loses to a robust 1/N or risk-parity book in live trading. Sophistication only pays when your inputs are reliable enough to feed it. When in doubt, prefer the robust, parameter-light method.
</Pitfall>

<KeyPoint title="Match the method to your trust in the data">
Track G is a ladder: 1/N → inverse-vol → risk parity → MVO, ordered by how much they trust your estimates. Shrinkage and Black-Litterman exist to make those estimates trustworthy. Pick the rung your data quality can support — and remember complexity is not edge.
</KeyPoint>
