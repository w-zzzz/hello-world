# Exponential Moving Average (EMA)

You just saw that the simple moving average treats every bar in its window with equal weight. The exponential moving average is the natural fix: weight recent prices more, weight older prices less, and let those weights decay smoothly as you go back in time. The result is a line that turns faster than the SMA on real moves while staying smooth enough to be useful.

In this lesson you will learn the recursive EMA formula, see why the smoothing constant $\alpha$ depends on a chosen period $n$, watch how the EMA responds to a step change in price, and meet the most common mistake — assuming "EMA(20)" and "SMA(20)" are comparable just because they share the number 20.

## Definition

The EMA is defined by a one-step recursion:

$$
\mathrm{EMA}_t = \alpha \, P_t + (1 - \alpha) \, \mathrm{EMA}_{t-1}
$$

where the smoothing constant $\alpha \in (0, 1)$ is chosen from a period $n$ by

$$
\alpha = \frac{2}{n + 1}.
$$

Each new bar's EMA is a blend: a fraction $\alpha$ of today's close plus a fraction $1 - \alpha$ of yesterday's EMA. Because yesterday's EMA already contained a blend of the day before, the influence of old prices does not disappear — it just shrinks by a factor of $(1-\alpha)$ every bar.

If you unroll the recursion, you can show the EMA is an infinite weighted sum where the weight on $P_{t-k}$ is $\alpha (1-\alpha)^k$. The weights decay geometrically and sum to 1.

## Why does $\alpha = 2/(n+1)$?

The choice is a convention, picked so that an EMA with period $n$ has roughly the same "center of mass" — the average age of the data it weights — as an SMA with the same $n$. For an SMA(20), the average lag is about 9.5 bars; setting $\alpha = 2/21 \approx 0.095$ in the EMA gives a similar effective lag. This is what lets people say "EMA(20)" and "SMA(20)" in the same sentence without immediately contradicting themselves.

## Step response: what happens when price jumps

Suppose price has been flat at 100 for a long time, so $\mathrm{EMA}_{t-1} = 100$. Then a piece of news pushes the close to 110 — a clean step up.

With $n = 20$, $\alpha \approx 0.0952$. The next EMA value is:

$$
\mathrm{EMA}_t = 0.0952 \times 110 + 0.9048 \times 100 = 100.95
$$

So the EMA absorbs about 9.5% of the move on bar 1. Iterating, it covers $1 - (1-\alpha)^k$ of the gap by bar $k$. After 20 bars the EMA has closed about $1 - 0.905^{20} \approx 86\%$ of the gap; after 40 bars, about $98\%$. The SMA(20) over the same path snaps fully into place at exactly bar 20 — but it bounces around discontinuously as old pre-jump bars exit the window one at a time. The EMA's response is smoother, even if it never *quite* arrives.

## Where EMA earns its keep

Because the EMA reacts faster to recent prices, it is the default choice when you care about catching turns earlier — momentum strategies, trend-following crossovers (e.g. EMA(12) vs EMA(26), the building block of MACD), and any setup where being a few bars late costs real basis points.

It is also nicer to compute online: you only need to keep the previous EMA value, not the whole window. For a streaming pipeline that processes millions of bars, that matters.

## What the EMA does not do

<Pitfall title="Same number, different meaning">
A common mistake is to assume EMA(20) and SMA(20) are interchangeable because both say "20." They have similar average lag by construction, but the EMA has an infinite memory: the price from 200 bars ago still contributes a tiny — but nonzero — amount to today's value. The SMA forgets exactly at bar 21. If you backtest an EMA-based strategy with an SMA-based parameter sweep, or vice versa, the period that "looked optimal" for one will not behave the same way in the other.

A related trap: when comparing across moving-average families, compare by **half-life** (the bar count $k$ where weights drop to half: $k = \ln(0.5) / \ln(1 - \alpha)$) instead of by nominal period. For EMA(20), the half-life is about 7 bars.
</Pitfall>

<KeyPoint title="The EMA contract">
EMA is a recursive weighted average where weights decay geometrically. Faster than SMA, smoother than the raw price, infinite memory, easy to compute online. Pick the period based on the response you want — and remember the period is a convention, not a hard window.
</KeyPoint>
