# Weighted Moving Average (WMA)

You have now seen two extremes. The SMA gives every bar in its window equal weight — simple, but blind to recency. The EMA gives recent bars more weight via geometric decay, with the side effect that *every* past bar still contributes a tiny amount forever. The weighted moving average sits between them: like the SMA, it has a finite window of exactly $n$ bars; like the EMA, it weights recent prices more heavily. The decay it uses is **linear**.

In this lesson you will see the WMA formula, why the linear weights normalize cleanly to $2/(n(n+1))$, when WMA is a sensible pick over SMA or EMA, and the trap that catches people who reach for the most "responsive" average without thinking about what they are responding to.

## Definition

For a window of $n$ bars, the WMA assigns weight $n$ to the most recent bar, $n-1$ to the one before, and so on down to weight $1$ for the oldest bar in the window:

$$
\mathrm{WMA}_t(n) = \frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} (n - i) \, P_{t-i}}{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} (n - i)}
$$

The denominator is the sum $1 + 2 + \cdots + n = n(n+1)/2$, so the formula simplifies to:

$$
\mathrm{WMA}_t(n) = \frac{2}{n(n+1)} \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} (n - i) \, P_{t-i}
$$

That tidy $2/(n(n+1))$ factor is just the normalizer that makes the weights add to 1.

A small worked example. Take $n = 4$ and the last four closes 100, 102, 104, 106 (oldest to newest). The weights from oldest to newest are $1, 2, 3, 4$, summing to 10. So:

$$
\mathrm{WMA}_4 = \frac{1 \cdot 100 + 2 \cdot 102 + 3 \cdot 104 + 4 \cdot 106}{10} = \frac{1040}{10} = 104.0
$$

By contrast the SMA(4) of the same series is $103.0$. The WMA is pulled higher because it weights the more recent (and higher) closes more.

## Where WMA lives between SMA and EMA

It helps to think in terms of weight profiles:

- **SMA(n)** — uniform weight $1/n$ on each of the last $n$ bars, zero before that.
- **WMA(n)** — linearly decaying weight on the last $n$ bars, zero before that.
- **EMA(n)** — geometrically decaying weight on **all** past bars, never exactly zero.

The WMA's effective lag is roughly $(n-1)/3$ bars, compared to $(n-1)/2$ for the SMA at the same $n$. So WMA(20) lags by about 6 bars on average, where SMA(20) lags by about 10. The EMA(20) lags by about 9.5 by construction. The WMA is the *most responsive of the three at the same nominal period*, while still forgetting old bars cleanly.

That makes WMA a reasonable pick when:

- You want responsiveness, but you do not want an indicator that "remembers" prices from a year ago.
- You prefer the WMA's straightforward weight shape over the EMA's recursive bookkeeping.

If you want to play with responsiveness interactively, the SMA and EMA playgrounds from B-01 and B-02 are the right sandboxes — switch the period there to feel how shorter windows trade smoothness for speed. The WMA's behavior sits between those two, closer to the EMA end.

## The responsiveness trap

<Pitfall title="More responsive is not the same as better signal">
A frequent mistake is to reach for the WMA (or any fast-reacting filter) because "it turns sooner than the SMA." Yes — and it also turns sooner on **fakeouts**. Every wiggle in the most recent bar gets weight $n$ out of $n(n+1)/2$, which for short windows is a large fraction. On a window of 10, the latest bar alone carries weight $10/55 \approx 18\%$; for SMA(10) it is exactly $10\%$.

If your underlying signal-to-noise ratio is low, switching SMA → WMA → EMA from least to most responsive can actually **lower** your strategy's Sharpe ratio, not raise it. The responsiveness you bought also amplifies noise. The right way to pick a moving average is by **what you want to filter out**, not by which one looks "smartest."
</Pitfall>

<KeyPoint title="The WMA contract">
WMA(n) is a finite window with linearly decaying weights, normalized by $2/(n(n+1))$. It lags less than SMA(n) and has cleaner finite memory than EMA(n) — but it amplifies recent noise more than either. Choose it when you want responsiveness *with* a hard cutoff on history.
</KeyPoint>
