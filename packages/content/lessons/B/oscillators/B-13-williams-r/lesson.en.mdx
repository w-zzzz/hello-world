# Williams %R

Larry Williams published %R (pronounced "percent R") in 1973. It measures the same thing as the Stochastic %K — where today's close sits inside the recent high-low range — but it flips the sign so the indicator lives between -100 and 0 instead of 0 and 100. Once you see the two formulas side by side, you cannot un-see that they are almost the same statistic.

In this lesson you will work through the formula, prove the algebraic relationship to Stochastic, and learn why mixing both indicators into one model is almost always a mistake.

## The formula

Let $\mathrm{HH}_n$ and $\mathrm{LL}_n$ be the highest high and lowest low over the last $n$ bars (default $n = 14$), and $C_t$ today's close:

$$
\%R = -100 \cdot \frac{\mathrm{HH}_n - C_t}{\mathrm{HH}_n - \mathrm{LL}_n}
$$

The numerator measures distance from today's close to the recent peak; the denominator is the range. So %R = 0 means today closed exactly at the recent high; %R = -100 means it closed at the recent low. The range is $[-100, 0]$.

Conventional thresholds:

- **Overbought:** $\%R > -20$ (close is near the top of the range).
- **Oversold:** $\%R < -80$ (close is near the bottom).

Note that "overbought" is the *higher* number here, because the scale is negative. This catches a lot of beginners.

## %R vs Stochastic %K — the algebra

Recall the Stochastic fast %K from B-11:

$$
\%K = 100 \cdot \frac{C_t - \mathrm{LL}_n}{\mathrm{HH}_n - \mathrm{LL}_n}
$$

Add %R and %K together:

$$
\%K + \%R = 100 \cdot \frac{(C_t - \mathrm{LL}_n) - (\mathrm{HH}_n - C_t)}{\mathrm{HH}_n - \mathrm{LL}_n} + \text{rearrange} \implies \%R = \%K - 100
$$

In other words, **Williams %R is just the raw fast Stochastic %K shifted down by 100**. There is no smoothing in %R (no built-in %D analog), but the underlying positional information is identical. If you plot them on top of each other, they trace the same shape — one runs 0 to 100, the other runs -100 to 0.

This is why you sometimes hear %R described as "inverted Stochastic without smoothing." If you have already mastered Stochastic, you have already mastered %R.

<KeyPoint title="What to remember">
%R is Stochastic %K minus 100. Range is [-100, 0]. Above -20 is overbought, below -80 is oversold. There is no built-in smoothing line, so the raw signal is jagged.
</KeyPoint>

## The double-counting trap

<Pitfall title="Do not feed Stochastic and %R into the same model">
Because $\%R = \%K - 100$, any linear model that takes both as features sees collinear columns — the design matrix is essentially singular. Tree models do not crash but will split on whichever ordering noise prefers, giving you fake importance. Worse, in a rules-based system you double-count the same signal: "%R below -80 AND Stochastic below 20" fires on identical bars.

If you want a momentum oscillator + a positional oscillator, pair Stochastic (or %R, pick one) with RSI or CCI — they answer different questions about the same price series. Picking both Stochastic and %R buys you nothing except the warm feeling that you are using more indicators.
</Pitfall>

## When %R is genuinely useful

Despite the redundancy with Stochastic, traders still reach for %R in two cases:

1. **You want the raw, unsmoothed signal.** Fast Stochastic + no SMA = exactly %R shifted up. Some chart packages expose this more cleanly through %R than through a "fast %K, no %D" Stochastic configuration.
2. **The negative scale matters for charting.** %R plotted on an axis where 0 is at the top reads more naturally for some people — extremes hit the top edge instead of running off into 100.

Neither reason has anything to do with edge. Both are about ergonomics.
