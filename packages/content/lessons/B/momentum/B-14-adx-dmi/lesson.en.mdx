# ADX and the Directional Movement Index (DMI)

Most indicators answer "which way is price going, and how hard?" Welles Wilder's 1978 Directional Movement System asks a different question: **is there a trend here at all?** Its DMI lines tell you **direction**, and ADX tells you **strength**. Together they help you decide whether trend-following is even the right tool today.

## Directional Movement: distilling direction from highs and lows

Wilder's first idea was to compress "today's direction" into two numbers. Compare today's high and low against yesterday's:

$$
+\mathrm{DM}_t = \begin{cases} H_t - H_{t-1}, & \text{if } H_t - H_{t-1} > L_{t-1} - L_t \text{ and positive} \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

$$
-\mathrm{DM}_t = \begin{cases} L_{t-1} - L_t, & \text{if } L_{t-1} - L_t > H_t - H_{t-1} \text{ and positive} \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

If today's high is more above yesterday's high than today's low is below yesterday's low, the excess becomes $+\mathrm{DM}$; otherwise the symmetric case gives $-\mathrm{DM}$. The two are never both positive — each bar votes for one direction or neither.

## From DM to DI: the Directional Indicators

Raw DM is noisy bar-to-bar, so Wilder normalizes by ATR (average true range) and smooths over $n$ periods (the canonical $n = 14$):

$$
+\mathrm{DI}_n = 100 \cdot \frac{\mathrm{EMA}_n(+\mathrm{DM})}{\mathrm{ATR}_n}, \quad -\mathrm{DI}_n = 100 \cdot \frac{\mathrm{EMA}_n(-\mathrm{DM})}{\mathrm{ATR}_n}
$$

A high $+\mathrm{DI}$ says upward bars dominate; a high $-\mathrm{DI}$ says the downside owns the tape. The classical trigger is a crossover — $+\mathrm{DI}$ rising above $-\mathrm{DI}$ for longs, the reverse for shorts.

## ADX: extracting strength from direction

DMI tells you which side is winning, but not by how much. Wilder solved that with a clever normalization:

$$
\mathrm{DX} = 100 \cdot \frac{|+\mathrm{DI} - (-\mathrm{DI})|}{+\mathrm{DI} + (-\mathrm{DI})}
$$

$$
\mathrm{ADX}_n = \mathrm{EMA}_n(\mathrm{DX}) \quad \text{(Wilder smoothing)}
$$

The absolute value is the point: DX discards direction and keeps only the *gap* between the two DI lines. When one side dominates, DX is large; when they tangle, DX collapses to zero. Smoothing DX with Wilder's EMA ($\alpha = 1/n$) gives ADX — a single trend-strength number.

<KeyPoint title="ADX measures trend strength, not direction">A rising ADX only says "the current direction is becoming more decisive." Whether that direction is up or down requires reading $+\mathrm{DI}$ vs $-\mathrm{DI}$. ADX on its own is direction-blind.</KeyPoint>

## Classic ADX thresholds

Widely cited starting points, not laws:

- **ADX < 20** — weak/absent trend. Trend strategies will likely whipsaw.
- **20 to 25** — ambiguous. Regime change forming, or a head-fake.
- **Above 25** — confirmed trend. Trend-following has its best edge.
- **Above 40-50** — very strong trend, but late. Fresh entries get worse risk/reward.

Wilder fit these on daily equities. Re-calibrate out-of-sample for your own instrument and timeframe.

## Putting it together

The common DMI+ADX workflow has three steps:

1. **Filter regime with ADX.** Only allow trend strategies when ADX > 25; below 20, switch to mean-reversion or stand aside.
2. **Pick a side with DI lines.** Choose long or short by whichever DI is higher.
3. **Time entries with DI crossovers.** Wait for $+\mathrm{DI}$ to actually cross above $-\mathrm{DI}$ before committing capital.

The idea: answer "should I be trading at all?" before "which way?" That order is what separates Wilder's system from naïve momentum chasing.

## Pitfalls

<Pitfall title="Using ADX below 20 as a buy-the-range signal">Beginners read "ADX < 20 = range" and flip it into "enter mean-reversion trades." But ADX is itself lagging — DX smoothed on already-smoothed DI inputs lags the regime by $n$ to $2n$ bars. By the time ADX prints 19, the range you wanted to fade is half-done; by the time it prints 26, the trend you wanted to ride has moved. ADX belongs in the regime *filter*, voting yes/no alongside price action, not in the trigger that fires the order.</Pitfall>

Two more common mistakes:

- **Treating a DI crossover as a standalone entry.** In ADX < 20 chop, DI lines cross constantly and each crossing costs commissions.
- **Reusing parameters across instruments.** Wilder's 14 was tuned on daily equities; the same 14 on 5-minute futures is a different beast.
