# Bollinger Bands: squeeze, mean-reversion, breakout

The previous lesson built Bollinger Bands from a rolling mean and a rolling standard deviation. Now we turn the indicator into trading rules. There are three classics — mean reversion, the squeeze breakout, and walking the bands — and every one of them works **only inside a specific regime**. The real skill is not the entry rule; it is knowing which regime you are in.

## 1. Mean reversion at the bands

The most intuitive trade: when price tags the upper band, short; when it tags the lower band, go long; cover at the middle line.

<MathBox>
$$
\text{entry}_t =
\begin{cases}
-1 & \text{if } P_t \ge \mathrm{Upper}_t \\
+1 & \text{if } P_t \le \mathrm{Lower}_t \\
\phantom{+}0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$
</MathBox>

This is the textbook strategy and it has an obvious appeal: trades are frequent, mechanical, and visually compelling.

It works when:

- Realised volatility is roughly stationary.
- There is no dominant trend on the scale of $n$ bars.
- Liquidity is deep enough that touching the band is not itself a signal of trouble.

It fails when any of those break. The same SPY band-touch rule that prints money on 2003-2007 daily data hands it back in 2008 and then refuses to recover until 2015. The bands did not stop working; the regime changed.

## 2. Walking the bands

In a strong trend, price hugs one band and pulls the middle line along with it. The same touch that screams "reversion" in a range now confirms **continuation**.

<IndicatorPlayground indicator="bollinger" />

Drag the chart to a trending segment and watch what happens. Closes consistently above the upper band — not just touching it — are a feature of momentum, not a bug. A useful filter: require **two consecutive closes outside the band on the same side** before you treat the touch as a reversion signal. In a real trend that filter triggers almost never; in a range it triggers often.

## 3. The squeeze and the breakout

When realised volatility collapses, bandwidth collapses with it. A "Bollinger squeeze" is conventionally defined as $\mathrm{BW}_t$ at, say, a six-month low. Squeezes do not predict *direction* — they predict that the **next leg will be larger than the last**. To turn that into a trade you need a direction trigger:

<MathBox>
$$
\text{long breakout}_t = \mathbf{1}\bigl[\mathrm{BW}_{t-1} \le q_{0.1}(\mathrm{BW})\bigr] \cdot \mathbf{1}\bigl[P_t > \mathrm{Upper}_{t-1}\bigr]
$$
</MathBox>

That is: only act on an upper-band break **after** a low-bandwidth setup. Combine with a volume confirmation (e.g. volume $> 1.5 \times$ its 20-bar average) and the false-break rate drops materially. Without those filters you are just buying every upper-band touch — the rule that already failed in §1.

## 4. Why every backtester rediscovers this

There is a folk theorem in quant blogs: every newcomer backtests band-touch mean reversion on SPY 2003-2007, finds a Sharpe near 2, scales it up, and loses money post-2008. Why is that specific period so flattering?

- **Volatility was structurally low and stationary.** A constant $\sigma$ assumption is roughly right.
- **Market microstructure was friendly.** Pre-HFT, pre-decimal-spread compression, mean-reversion edges decayed slowly.
- **Macro regime was a long bull range.** No dominant trend on the band's timescale.

Post-2008 the VIX regime broke, intraday auto-correlation flipped, and Fed-driven trends ran for months. The bands themselves are unchanged — but the **environment** that made the rule profitable disappeared.

<Pitfall>
Every generation of backtester rediscovers band-touch mean reversion on SPY 2003-2007 and loses money on it from 2008 onward. The cause is a **vol-regime change**, not a bug in Bollinger Bands. Always condition your rules on a regime detector — bandwidth percentile, realised vol, or a trend filter — before you size up.
</Pitfall>

## 5. A regime-aware playbook

A practical, defensible recipe:

1. Compute $\mathrm{BW}_t$ and its rolling percentile over the last 252 bars.
2. **Low percentile (< 20%)**: arm a breakout rule. Trade the first close outside the band, with volume confirmation. Stop on a return inside the band.
3. **Mid percentile + flat SMA slope**: arm mean reversion. Fade band touches towards the middle line. Skip if the SMA is sloping noticeably.
4. **High percentile + steep SMA slope**: assume "walking the bands". Do not fade; consider trend-following on pullbacks to the middle line.

The same indicator drives all three. The regime classifier — not the entry rule — is what determines whether you make or lose money over a decade.

<KeyPoint>
Bollinger Bands do not have a single strategy. They have a **family** of strategies, each tied to a volatility/trend regime. Build the regime classifier first; the entry rule is the easy part.
</KeyPoint>

<Reveal title="A worked example: NDX 2020">
Late February 2020: bandwidth in the 5th percentile after months of grinding rally. First close below the lower band on Feb 24 with volume 2.3× the 20-bar average. A regime-aware system flips from mean-reversion mode to breakout-short mode and stays short until $\mathrm{BW}_t$ peaks in late March. A naive band-touch reverter, in contrast, is long all the way down.
</Reveal>
