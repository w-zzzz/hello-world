# MACD: the line and the signal

The Moving Average Convergence Divergence indicator — MACD for short — is the most popular momentum tool in technical analysis. It is also one of the most misunderstood. People stare at the two wiggly lines, wait for a "cross," and treat the result as a trading signal. That is a recipe for buying every bounce and selling every dip late.

In this lesson you will learn what the MACD line actually measures, why the signal line exists, and how to read the classic three states of the indicator without falling for its built-in lag.

## Definition: the difference of two EMAs

MACD is built from the EMA you already know. At time $t$ on a price series $P_t$:

$$
\mathrm{MACD}_t = \mathrm{EMA}_{12}(P_t) - \mathrm{EMA}_{26}(P_t)
$$

The signal line is just another EMA — this time of the MACD line itself:

$$
\mathrm{Signal}_t = \mathrm{EMA}_9(\mathrm{MACD}_t)
$$

The classic parameters are $(12, 26, 9)$. Gerald Appel chose them in the 1970s for daily charts, and they have stuck. They are not magic; they are a reasonable compromise between speed and smoothness.

## Intuition: momentum of momentum

Think about what $\mathrm{EMA}_{12} - \mathrm{EMA}_{26}$ measures. The fast EMA tracks recent price closely; the slow EMA reacts more sluggishly. When the fast line pulls ahead of the slow line, recent prices are systematically higher than the longer-term average — that is up momentum. When the fast line falls behind, momentum is down.

So MACD is a **momentum-of-momentum indicator**: a smoothed-out estimate of how strongly price is accelerating in one direction. It oscillates around zero, with no fixed bounds — unlike RSI, MACD can drift arbitrarily far from the centerline in a strong trend.

The signal line then smooths the MACD itself. Its purpose is not to predict — it is to give you a slower reference so that crossovers are less twitchy.

## The three classic states

Reading MACD comes down to three observations you can make at a glance.

1. **MACD > 0 and rising.** The 12-EMA is above the 26-EMA and the gap is widening. This is the canonical "strong uptrend" state — momentum is up, and accelerating.
2. **MACD crosses above the signal line.** A bullish trigger. Many systems use this as an entry. Be honest: by the time the cross prints, the underlying move is often half-done.
3. **MACD crosses below the signal line.** The mirror image — a bearish trigger, often late, but useful as a discipline tool to exit longs.

## What MACD is not

<Pitfall title="MACD is a lagging confirmation, not a forecast">
MACD is an EMA of price differences smoothed by another EMA. Mathematically, it is filtered twice. By the time the signal-line cross prints, the underlying move is often already mature. Treat a MACD cross as a way to **confirm** an idea you formed from price structure — not as a standalone "buy this candle" trigger.
</Pitfall>

A related trap: people see MACD crosses on a 5-minute chart and conclude the indicator does not work. It does — but on noisy intraday data the $(12, 26, 9)$ defaults fire constantly, most of them false. Either retune the lengths or step up the timeframe.

<KeyPoint title="Mental model to carry forward">
MACD measures the gap between a fast and a slow EMA. Positive and rising means up momentum. Crosses against the signal line are confirmations, not predictions, and they lag the underlying move.
</KeyPoint>
