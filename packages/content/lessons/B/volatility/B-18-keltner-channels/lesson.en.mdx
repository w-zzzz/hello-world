# Keltner Channels

A Keltner Channel is a volatility envelope drawn around a moving average. It looks superficially like a Bollinger Band, but the math underneath differs in a way that matters: Keltner uses **ATR** (absolute, gap-aware range), while Bollinger uses the **standard deviation of close prices**. Once you see what each formula throws away, you can pick the right one.

In this lesson you will write the Keltner formula, compare it with Bollinger, and see why touches on a Keltner band rarely mean what touches on a Bollinger band mean.

## Definition

Keltner uses three lines: an EMA midline plus an ATR-scaled envelope on each side. With $n = 20$, $m = 10$, $k = 2$ as typical defaults:

$$
\mathrm{Mid}_t = \mathrm{EMA}_n(C_t)
$$

$$
\mathrm{Upper}_t = \mathrm{Mid}_t + k \cdot \mathrm{ATR}_m, \qquad \mathrm{Lower}_t = \mathrm{Mid}_t - k \cdot \mathrm{ATR}_m
$$

The midline tracks **trend**, the band width tracks **typical bar travel**. Because ATR uses Wilder smoothing and includes gap risk, the envelope reacts to overnight jumps that a stddev-of-close measure can miss.

## Keltner vs Bollinger — the same shape, different physics

Both indicators are "moving-average + width," but they answer different questions about width.

Picture a market that gaps down \$5 every morning and drifts flat all day. The closes trend smoothly, so rolling stddev stays small and Bollinger says "calm." But ATR is large every day because TR catches the gap — Keltner widens. **ATR sees overnight risk; stddev of close does not.**

## When to prefer which

Use **Bollinger** for *how unusual* recent closes are versus their short-term distribution — a statistical mean-reversion framing.

Use **Keltner** for *how big bars are* in real price units, including overnight risk — a trader's noise-floor framing. Trend channels and ATR stops are usually Keltner-shaped.

Many systems use both: Bollinger inside Keltner ("squeeze") flags an unusually quiet variance regime relative to typical range — compressed energy before a breakout.

## How traders use it

Three idioms cover most cases.

1. **Trend channel.** Closes persistently pushing along the upper band is a strong-trend signal, not an overbought one. The midline is dynamic support for adds.
2. **Volatility breakout.** When price exits the band after a tight stretch (low ATR), the move often runs further than a Bollinger touch would suggest — Keltner's slow band means the system was genuinely sleepy.
3. **Stop placement.** Trailing stops at $\mathrm{Mid}_t - k \cdot \mathrm{ATR}_m$ give a volatility-adjusted exit on the chart you are already watching.

## Pitfalls

<Pitfall title="Treating Keltner touches like Bollinger touches">
On Bollinger, a band touch is by construction a high-percentile event in the recent close distribution — mean-reversion traders read it as "stretched." On Keltner, the upper band is just "EMA plus 2 ATRs," and because ATR is Wilder-smoothed, **price can ride or sit outside the upper band for many bars in a strong trend.** Fading every Keltner touch is one of the fastest ways to be run over on a trend day. If you trade Keltner mean-reversion, demand extra confirmation (e.g. RSI divergence).
</Pitfall>

<KeyPoint title="Carry this forward">
Keltner = EMA midline + ATR-scaled envelope. ATR makes the envelope **gap-aware and slow**, which is great for trend channels and stops, but it does *not* make band touches statistically extreme the way Bollinger touches are. Pick the indicator that matches the question you are asking.
</KeyPoint>
