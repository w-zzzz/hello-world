# Donchian Channels

The Donchian Channel is the simplest possible volatility envelope: take the highest high and lowest low over the last $n$ bars and draw them. No smoothing, no exponents, no standard deviations — only the raw extremes of recent history. That bluntness is what makes it useful, and what makes it dangerous.

By the end you will know how to compute Donchian Channels, why they powered the Turtle breakout system, and why "new 20-day high" is **by construction a chase**.

## Definition

For a window length $n$ (typically 20), the upper, lower, and middle Donchian lines at bar $t$ are:

$$
\mathrm{Upper}_t = \max(H_{t-n+1}, H_{t-n+2}, \ldots, H_t)
$$

$$
\mathrm{Lower}_t = \min(L_{t-n+1}, L_{t-n+2}, \ldots, L_t)
$$

$$
\mathrm{Mid}_t = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\mathrm{Upper}_t + \mathrm{Lower}_t\bigr)
$$

That is the whole indicator. Upper is the **rolling $n$-bar high**, lower the rolling $n$-bar low, middle the midpoint. No averaging, no weighting. The upper line is a staircase that only ever rises or stays flat until an old high rolls off the window.

Imagine, at each bar, sliding a 20-bar window and recording max high and min low. The upper line traces every fresh 20-day high — and only updates **after** price prints a new extreme.

## Why this is a "breakout" indicator

Most indicators are descriptive — RSI describes momentum, ATR describes range. Donchian is **definitional**: a Donchian upper-band breakout *is* a new $n$-bar high. Wait for the line to lift, buy the close, and your entry is by definition preceded by an extreme. No Donchian buy signal can arrive *before* the extreme it describes.

That makes Donchian a clean specification: "buy when the close is above the prior 20-day Donchian upper, exit on a new 10-day low." It is also trivial to backtest — only the window length matters.

## The Turtle system

The most famous use of Donchian is the Turtle Trading system Richard Dennis taught in 1983. The entry/exit core was nested Donchian channels:

- **Entry:** buy on a new 20-day high (55-day in System 2).
- **Exit:** sell on a new 10-day low (20-day in System 2).

The asymmetric window — wider for entry, tighter for exit — locks profits while letting winners run. Position size was ATR-targeted, which is the bridge to your previous lesson: **Donchian decides when, ATR decides how much.** Without ATR sizing, the bare breakout rule gets shredded by chop.

## A small worked example

Suppose SPY's last 20 daily highs include a \$502 from 15 days ago, with the rest at or below \$500. Today's high is \$501, so $\mathrm{Upper}_t = 502$ — unchanged. Five days later the \$502 rolls out. If the next 19-max is \$500.50 and today's high is \$501.20, the upper band drops to \$501.20 — a fresh high in the *current* window despite being below \$502. This stair-step shape is the entire reason traders talk about "Donchian breakouts."

## What Donchian does not tell you

Three things every user must internalize.

1. **No volatility scaling.** A 1-point break in a 30-cent ATR regime and in a \$5 ATR regime print identically — only one is meaningful.
2. **Persistent latency.** The signal is never early. You always buy after the new high.
3. **Stair-step path dependence.** The upper band only falls when an old extreme rolls off, so it can sit flat far above current price for many bars. That gap tempts "anticipatory" trades — they are not Donchian signals.

## Pitfalls

<Pitfall title="Donchian is a chase indicator">
By construction, a Donchian breakout fires **after** price hits a new $n$-bar extreme — you are always buying the move, never anticipating it. In a clean trend that is fine; in chop you will buy every false high and sell every false low, paying the gap each time. A bare Donchian rule without (a) volatility-aware sizing, (b) a regime filter, or (c) a longer trend filter is one of the easiest ways to bleed an account. The Turtles did not get rich on Donchian alone — Donchian *plus* ATR sizing *plus* strict risk limits did the work.
</Pitfall>

<KeyPoint title="Carry this forward">
Donchian = rolling $n$-bar high/low. It is the cleanest possible definition of "breakout," but it is **a description of an event that already happened**. Marry it to volatility-aware sizing and a regime filter, or it will chase you into every chop.
</KeyPoint>
