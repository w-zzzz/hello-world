# Look-ahead bias in indicator computation

If your backtest equity curve looks gorgeous, the first thing to check is not the indicator — it's whether the signal at time $t$ accidentally used information from after $t$. That bug is called **look-ahead bias**, and it is the most common reason "great" strategies stop working the moment you take them live. This lesson catalogs the four shapes it takes in real codebases.

## The discipline: time-respecting indicators

A signal $s_t$ is only honest if it satisfies the **causality constraint**:

$$
s_t = g\bigl(\mathcal{F}_t\bigr), \quad \mathcal{F}_t = \sigma\bigl(P_u, V_u : u \leq t\bigr)
$$

where $\mathcal{F}_t$ is the information set available **at or before** time $t$. The moment $g$ touches anything dated $> t$, your backtest is fiction. Each trap below is a flavor of that one rule being broken.

## Trap 1: Same-bar execution of a same-bar signal

The textbook version. You compute a signal on bar $t$'s close, then assume you can act on bar $t$'s close at the same instant.

If your fill engine has no concept of "next bar," at minimum lag the signal by one bar. The "obvious" version inflates returns because every trade enters at the same close it predicted.

## Trap 2: Centered rolling windows

Pandas will happily compute `rolling(window=21, center=True).mean()`. The window is centered on $t$, which means it uses bars from $t-10$ to $t+10$. Visually the line tracks price beautifully. In production it is a fantasy.

$$
\widehat{\mu}_t^{\mathrm{centered}} = \frac{1}{21} \sum_{k=-10}^{10} P_{t+k}
$$

Every $k > 0$ term is in the future. Never use `center=True` outside of exploratory data analysis, and never let a centered series leak into a strategy.

## Trap 3: Restated data

You pull yfinance daily bars. Six weeks later you pull the same range again — and yesterday's close is slightly different. Dividends, splits, and late corrections all change historical data after the fact. A backtest run today includes restatements that a strategy running in 2023 could not have known.

The mitigation is **point-in-time data**: store the snapshot you would have had at $t$, and replay backtests against that snapshot, not the latest one.

## Trap 4: Survivorship bias

Backtesting "the current S&P 500" on data from 2010 is a look-ahead too. The current index does not contain Lehman, WaMu, Bear Stearns, or General Motors. By selecting your universe **with knowledge of who survived**, you have peeked at the future.

A symbol-set $U_t$ is honest only if its membership at time $t$ depends solely on $\mathcal{F}_t$:

$$
U_t = h\bigl(\mathcal{F}_t\bigr)
$$

A 15-year backtest on today's QQQ list dramatically overstates returns. The right universe is the list as it stood at $t$, including the names that later got delisted.

## The chart smell test

Look at one period of your equity curve.

<Chart symbol="SPY" height={320} intervalLabel="D" />

If your strategy has a Sharpe over 5 on a low-frequency price-only signal, that is not skill — it's almost certainly a leak. Real long-only equity strategies on liquid US stocks live in the 0.3–1.0 Sharpe range. Look-ahead is the only thing that reliably manufactures Sharpe 5.

## Pitfall

<Pitfall title="Beautiful backtests are guilty until proven innocent">
Look-ahead bias produces equity curves so clean they feel like a reward for hard work. They are not. Before celebrating, run this checklist: did the signal at $t$ touch any $P_u$ with $u > t$? Did any rolling window use `center=True`? Was the universe selected with hindsight? Was the data restated after the fact? If even one answer is yes, the result is invalid.
</Pitfall>

<KeyPoint title="The lag-by-one habit">
For every signal you build, default to lagging it by one bar before computing returns: `position = signal.shift(1)`. This single line eliminates the most common form of look-ahead. If your strategy stops being profitable when you do this, you never had a strategy — you had a measurement of yesterday's prices on tomorrow's positions.
</KeyPoint>
