# RSI divergence: bullish, bearish, and the over-fitting trap

In B-06 you learned that "RSI > 70 means sell" is a losing rule on its own. In B-07 you learned how RSI is actually computed. This lesson covers the most interesting (and most over-claimed) RSI pattern: **divergence** between price and the indicator.

By the end, you'll be able to define bullish and bearish divergence precisely, you'll know why even a textbook-perfect divergence is a *sign* and not a *signal*, and you'll have a recipe for validating any divergence rule on out-of-sample data instead of cherry-picking it on a chart.

## The two flavors of divergence

Divergence means price and RSI disagree about the direction of a recent swing.

**Bearish divergence.** Price prints a higher high than its previous swing high, but RSI prints a *lower* high. The new price peak was made on weaker momentum than the old one. Buyers are tiring even as price grinds up.

$$
P_2 > P_1 \quad \text{and} \quad \mathrm{RSI}_2 < \mathrm{RSI}_1
$$

where $P_1, P_2$ are consecutive swing highs in price and $\mathrm{RSI}_1, \mathrm{RSI}_2$ are RSI at those same highs.

**Bullish divergence.** Price prints a lower low than its previous swing low, but RSI prints a *higher* low. The new low came on less downside momentum than the old. Sellers are tiring.

$$
P_2 < P_1 \quad \text{and} \quad \mathrm{RSI}_2 > \mathrm{RSI}_1
$$

In both cases the indicator is telling you something the raw price chart hides: the strength of the move has changed even though its direction hasn't (yet).

<KeyPoint title="Divergence in one sentence">
Divergence = price extends the trend while RSI fails to confirm it. Bearish: higher price high + lower RSI high. Bullish: lower price low + higher RSI low.
</KeyPoint>

You'll also notice something less convenient: many of those divergences resolve *eventually*, but the eventual resolution is often weeks away. Some never resolve — price just keeps going. That's the next section.

## Divergence is a sign, not a signal

A divergence is information about momentum, not a timestamped trade instruction. Three uncomfortable facts:

1. **Divergences persist.** A bearish divergence on the daily chart can survive three more weeks of new highs. Each new high makes the divergence "look even better" in hindsight while shorting it has cost you 10%.
2. **Divergences fail.** Sometimes a divergence forms and then price simply continues. Momentum reasserts. The pattern was real; the trade still lost.
3. **Divergences are sparse.** Clean swings with clearly comparable peaks don't form every week. If your strategy needs 100 trades a year, divergence alone won't get you there.

The pros use divergences as a **filter** layered on top of a triggering event. The trigger is usually one of:

- A break of a recent swing low/high (price confirms the divergence).
- A close back through a moving average (e.g. 20-EMA).
- A close back through a previously broken support or resistance level.

The divergence answers "is the prevailing trend losing strength?" The trigger answers "has the market actually started to move yet?" Without the trigger, you're trading a forecast. With the trigger, you're trading a fact.

<Pitfall title="Drawing divergences after the fact is the easiest way to fool yourself">
On any chart, in any decade, you can post-hoc find a divergence right before every turn. The chart is a stationary picture; you know where the top was. This proves nothing. To test whether a divergence rule has edge, you must (1) write it down as code that only looks at past bars, (2) generate the signals on data your eyes have never seen, and (3) measure the hit rate, average return, and time-to-resolution. Track C lesson C-04 walks through this in a game format. Until you've done that work, treat hand-drawn divergences on the daily chart as decoration, not evidence.
</Pitfall>

## A minimal validation recipe

Here is a pseudocode-level test you can run yourself once you've finished Track C. It is intentionally strict because that's the only way to avoid fooling yourself:

1. **Define swings algorithmically.** A swing high is a bar whose high exceeds the previous $k$ and next $k$ bars' highs (e.g. $k = 5$). Same for swing lows. No eyeballing.
2. **Define divergence algorithmically.** Walk the swing-high series. A bearish divergence triggers when `swing_high[i].price > swing_high[i-1].price` AND `rsi_at(swing_high[i]) < rsi_at(swing_high[i-1])`.
3. **Split the data.** Use 2010–2018 to pick parameters ($n$, $k$, RSI period). Use 2019–2024 as out-of-sample. Never look at out-of-sample numbers until the rule is frozen.
4. **Measure.** On out-of-sample data, what fraction of triggers lead to a $\geq 2\%$ adverse move within 20 bars? What's the maximum drawdown of a strategy that shorts every bearish divergence and covers $N$ bars later?
5. **Compare.** Run the same measurement on **random entry signals** at the same frequency. If your divergence rule isn't visibly better than random, it isn't a rule — it's a story.

Most informal divergence rules fail step 5. That's not a reason to abandon RSI; it's a reason to combine divergence with other filters (regime, volume, confirmation candles) before claiming a signal.

## Why we still teach it

If divergence is this fragile, why bother? Because the underlying logic — "momentum is leading or lagging price" — is real and shows up across many indicators (MACD histogram, OBV, even raw volume). RSI divergence is the cleanest place to learn the *pattern of thinking*. Once you can recognize and validate it on RSI, you can recognize and validate it on any oscillator.
