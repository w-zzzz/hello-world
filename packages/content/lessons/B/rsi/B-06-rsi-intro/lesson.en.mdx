# RSI: intuition and the overbought/oversold trap

The Relative Strength Index (RSI), introduced by J. Welles Wilder in 1978, is a **bounded momentum oscillator**. It compresses recent gains and losses into a single number between 0 and 100. That bounding is what makes it easy to read — and easy to misuse.

In this lesson you'll build intuition for what RSI actually measures, why the [0, 100] range matters, and why "RSI > 70 means sell" is the single most expensive mistake new traders make.

## What RSI is measuring

Strip the formula away and RSI answers one question:

> Over the last $n$ bars, how much of the total price movement was **upward** vs **downward**?

If every bar of the lookback window was up, RSI prints 100. If every bar was down, RSI prints 0. Anything in between is a ratio — the share of the recent move that pushed price higher.

The default lookback is $n = 14$ bars. That is Wilder's original choice and remains the industry default. Shorter periods (7, 9) make RSI twitchier; longer ones (21, 28) smooth it.

<KeyPoint title="The one-line definition">
RSI is the fraction of recent price action that was upward, rescaled to [0, 100]. It does not predict direction — it summarizes what just happened.
</KeyPoint>

## Why bounded matters

Most indicators are unbounded: a moving average on a \$30 stock and a SMA on a \$3,000 stock live on different scales. You can't pool them or set a universal threshold.

RSI lives in [0, 100] **for every instrument, on every timeframe**. An RSI of 78 on SPY and an RSI of 78 on a microcap mean the same thing structurally — recent action has been heavily one-sided. The bounding comes from the formula itself, derived next lesson:

$$
0 \leq \mathrm{RSI}_t \leq 100 \quad \text{for all } t
$$

## Wilder's thresholds: 70 and 30

Wilder proposed two reference lines:

- **70** — above this, the instrument is **overbought**: recent action has been mostly up.
- **30** — below this, the instrument is **oversold**: recent action has been mostly down.

Notice the careful language. Overbought does **not** mean "about to fall." It means "the last 14 bars have been dominated by buyers." Whether the next bar is up or down is governed by what comes next — not by what just happened.

## The trap: "RSI > 70, so sell"

The most common beginner rule is:

> When RSI > 70, short the stock. When RSI < 30, buy it.

This rule loses money in trending markets — and trending markets are where the biggest moves live. Consider a stock in a strong uptrend. RSI rises to 75, then 80, then 85, and the stock keeps going. The trader who sold at the first cross of 70 watches it climb another 20% before any pullback.

Real SPY example: during the late-2020 melt-up, daily RSI held above 70 for **six straight weeks** while price advanced about 14%. A naive "sell at 70" rule would have shorted on day one and stopped out repeatedly all the way up.

<Pitfall title="Overbought is not a sell signal">
RSI above 70 means momentum is strong — not that it's about to reverse. In trends RSI can stay above 70 (or below 30) for weeks. Use the thresholds as a **state label**, not a standalone trade signal. We'll combine RSI with confirmation in lesson B-08.
</Pitfall>

## How to actually use RSI thresholds

If "sell at 70" is wrong, what's right? Three safer reframings:

1. **Range-bound markets only.** In a sideways instrument, mean-reversion off 70/30 has historical edge. The hard part is identifying "range-bound" — that's regime detection (Track C).
2. **Look for failure swings.** Wilder's original signal was "RSI hits 70+, pulls back below 70, fails to reclaim it on the next rally." Two-step, much cleaner.
3. **Combine with divergence.** When price makes a new high but RSI doesn't, the rally is losing steam. Topic of lesson B-08.

None of these are guarantees. They are filters that turn a noisy raw signal into something with better odds.
