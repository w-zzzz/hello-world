# Portfolio heat

Per-trade sizing keeps any single loss small. But you rarely hold one position. Portfolio heat is the total open risk across *all* your positions at once — and it is the number that decides how bad your worst day can get. A book of ten trades, each risking a 'safe' 1%, feels diversified. Whether it actually is depends entirely on how those ten move together.

This lesson defines heat, shows how correlation changes the math, and explains the crash scenario that catches over-extended traders.

## Defining heat

Portfolio heat is the sum of the open risk on every position — for each, the dollars you would lose if its stop triggered, expressed as a fraction of equity:

$$
H = \sum_{i=1}^{n} r_i
$$

where $r_i$ is position $i$'s risk as a fraction of equity. If you hold 8 positions each risking 1%, your nominal heat is 8%. Many traders set a heat cap — a maximum total open risk, say 6% — and stop adding positions once it is reached, no matter how good the next signal looks. The cap bounds the loss on a day when *everything* goes wrong at once.

## Correlation changes everything

The simple sum $H = \sum r_i$ assumes positions move independently. They do not. If positions are correlated, their risks add up faster than the sum suggests, because they tend to lose together. A rough portfolio-risk expression for two equal positions with correlation $\rho$ is:

$$
\sigma_p^2 = r_1^2 + r_2^2 + 2\,\rho\,r_1 r_2
$$

When $\rho = 0$ the cross term vanishes and risks combine in quadrature (less than the linear sum). When $\rho = 1$ the two positions are effectively one doubled position, and the risk is the *full* linear sum. Correlation is the dial between 'diversified' and 'one big bet wearing ten costumes.'

## Heat budgeting in practice

Because correlated positions consume more real risk than their nominal sum, a sound rule counts correlated trades as partly the same trade. Three long tech stocks in an uptrend are not three independent 1% bets; treat them closer to one 2–3% bet. Set your heat cap on *effective* risk, group positions by what actually drives them, and leave room for the correlations you cannot see.

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall title="Ten 1% positions are not 10% when they are correlated">
The fatal assumption is that summing nominal risks gives your true exposure. If your ten positions are correlated — and in a crisis nearly all risk assets are — they do not lose a little independently; they lose together. Worse, correlations are not stable: in a crash they converge toward 1, so the diversification you counted on evaporates exactly when you need it. Ten 'independent' 1% bets can deliver a 10%-plus loss in a single coordinated move. Budget heat on effective, correlation-aware risk, and assume correlations rise under stress.
</Pitfall>

<KeyPoint title="Heat is correlation-weighted, not a simple sum">
Portfolio heat is total open risk across positions. Independent risks combine in quadrature; correlated risks add closer to linearly, and in a crash correlations go to 1 so losses approach the full sum. Cap effective heat, group correlated trades, and leave margin for correlation spikes.
</KeyPoint>
