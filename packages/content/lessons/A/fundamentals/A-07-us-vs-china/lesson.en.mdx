# US vs China A-share markets

So far Track A has described "the market" as if it works the same everywhere. It does not. The rules that decide *when* you can trade, *how fast* you can round-trip, and *which* directions you are even allowed to bet differ from country to country. Get this wrong and a strategy that looks brilliant in a backtest can be flat-out illegal — or mechanically impossible — in the market you actually trade.

This lesson contrasts two of the biggest equity markets a quant will meet: US equities and China A-shares. The goal is not to memorize regulations but to feel how microstructure shapes what your code is allowed to do.

## Settlement: T+0 intraday vs T+1

In US equities you can buy and sell the same share within the same session as many times as you like (subject to pattern-day-trader rules on margin). Effectively the position is yours the instant the trade fills, so intraday round-trips are normal.

China A-shares settle **T+1**: shares bought today cannot be sold until the next trading day. Cash from a sale is also not withdrawable until T+1. This single rule deletes an entire family of strategies — anything that opens and closes a long position inside one day simply cannot run on A-shares.

## Price limits: free float vs daily caps

US stocks have no fixed daily price limit. Circuit breakers can halt the whole market on extreme moves, but an individual name can rise or fall 30% in a day if the orders are there.

China A-shares enforce a **daily price limit** — typically $\pm 10\%$ for main-board names (and $\pm 5\%$ for stocks under special treatment, wider bands on STAR/ChiNext). When a stock is "limit-up," sellers vanish and the order book is one-sided:

$$
P^{\text{high}}_t \le (1 + \ell)\, C_{t-1}, \qquad \ell = 0.10
$$

where $C_{t-1}$ is the prior close and $\ell$ is the limit fraction. On a limit-up day you often *cannot buy at all* — there is no offer to lift.

## Shorting and lot sizes

Shorting US equities is broadly available: borrow the shares, sell, buy back later. China restricts shorting heavily — only designated securities, through margin-trading accounts, with limited borrow. For most retail A-share traders, "going short" is not an option.

Order size differs too. China trades in a **lot** (一手) of 100 shares: buy orders must be in multiples of 100. US markets allow single-share and even fractional orders.

## Common pitfalls

<Pitfall title="Assuming US microstructure applies everywhere">
A backtest that round-trips intraday is fine on US equities but **illegal on A-shares** under T+1 — the engine fills a sell that could never happen. Worse, on a limit-up day your "buy" fills at a price where no real seller existed. If you trade A-shares, your backtest must enforce T+1 holding and refuse fills against a locked limit. Track E's E-15 builds these mechanics out in detail.
</Pitfall>

<KeyPoint title="Microstructure is part of the strategy">
Settlement (T+0 vs T+1), price limits ($\pm10\%$), shorting access, and lot size are not trivia — they decide which strategies are even *possible*. Always know the rules of the market you are coding for, not the market you read about.
</KeyPoint>
