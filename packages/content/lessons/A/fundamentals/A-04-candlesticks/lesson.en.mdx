# Reading a candlestick chart

In the last lesson you learned how an order becomes a fill. Every fill prints a price, and over a fixed window — a minute, a day, a week — those prices get compressed into a single drawing: the candlestick. Almost every chart you will ever look at as a quant is a wall of these candles. This lesson is about reading one candle honestly, before you ever try to read patterns into a row of them.

A candle is not a signal. It is a four-number summary of what happened in one period. Learn to see those four numbers and you will already be ahead of most people staring at the same screen.

## The four numbers: OHLC

Each candle summarizes one period with four prices:

- **Open (O)** — the first traded price of the period.
- **High (H)** — the highest price reached.
- **Low (L)** — the lowest price reached.
- **Close (C)** — the last traded price of the period.

Two simple quantities describe the shape of any candle:

$$
\text{range} = H - L, \qquad \text{body} = |C - O|
$$

The **range** is the full vertical extent of the candle — total ground covered. The **body** is the distance between open and close — the net result. The difference between them lives in the **wicks** (also called shadows), the thin lines above and below the body.

## Body vs wicks

The body is drawn as a filled rectangle from open to close. The wicks are thin lines stretching to the high and the low.

- A **long body** means open and close were far apart: the period had a decisive net move.
- A **small body** means price ended near where it began, regardless of how far it travelled in between.
- A **long upper wick** means price pushed up but was sold back down before the close — buyers tried, sellers answered.
- A **long lower wick** means price dropped but was bought back up before the close.

So a candle with a tiny body and two long wicks travelled a long range but went nowhere net — a tug-of-war that ended in a draw.

## Bullish vs bearish, and the doji

Colour encodes direction. By convention a **bullish** candle (close above open) is drawn hollow or green; a **bearish** candle (close below open) is filled or red. That single bit of colour tells you the sign of $C - O$, nothing more.

A **doji** is a candle whose open and close are almost equal, so the body nearly vanishes. It says the period opened and closed at the same place: indecision, or balance between buyers and sellers. A doji after a long run is often read as hesitation — but read, not obeyed.

## What one candle can and cannot tell you

A single candle is a low-information event. It tells you four prices and nothing about *why*. It does not show the order book, the spread, who was buying, or whether the volume was real. A dramatic-looking candle on a thin, illiquid tape can be one large order — not a crowd.

<Pitfall title="Candlestick patterns are widely over-trusted">
Named patterns — hammer, engulfing, shooting star — feel like signals because they have memorable shapes and names. But a single candle, or even a pair, is a tiny sample. The same shape that 'works' in an uptrend fails in a chop. What actually matters is context: the prevailing trend, whether the candle sits at a meaningful level, and whether volume confirms it. The shape is the least informative part. Treat patterns as a vocabulary for describing what happened, not as a recipe for predicting what happens next.
</Pitfall>

<KeyPoint title="Read the numbers, not the name">
Every candle is just open, high, low, close over one period. Body shows the net move, wicks show the rejected extremes. Before you name a pattern, ask what the four numbers are and what context surrounds them.
</KeyPoint>
