# What happens when you click "buy"?

When you tap "buy" in a broker app, a lot more happens than "I now own the thing." Your broker has to translate your intent into a specific instruction the exchange understands: how many shares, at what price (if any), under what conditions, and what to do if those conditions cannot be met. That instruction is called an **order**, and the type you pick determines your fills, your costs, and your risks.

This lesson walks through the four order types every quant must know, plus a quick tour of the institutional toolbox. By the end you will have a decision tree you can apply on every trade.

## Market order — speed first

A **market order** says: "buy (or sell) this quantity, right now, at whatever price the book offers."

- Pro: virtually guaranteed to fill, immediately.
- Con: zero control over price. You pay the ask (buy) or hit the bid (sell), and if your size is bigger than the top level you walk the book.

Use market orders when speed matters more than the last cent — for example, exiting a position when your stop signal has fired and you do not want to risk missing the move.

## Limit order — price first

A **limit order** says: "buy at $P$ or better, sell at $P$ or better — otherwise, wait."

- Pro: full control over the price you pay or receive.
- Con: no guarantee of a fill. If the market never trades at your limit, your order sits forever (or expires at the close, depending on its **time-in-force**).

A buy limit at the bid means you are joining the queue of liquidity providers. You earn the spread if filled, but you take **adverse selection** risk: you are most likely to get filled exactly when the price is about to move against you.

```python
# Pseudocode for a passive buy strategy.
def place_passive_buy(symbol, size, best_bid):
    return submit_limit_order(symbol, side="buy",
                              price=best_bid, quantity=size,
                              tif="DAY")
```

## Stop order

A **stop order** is a market order armed with a trigger. "If the price trades at or through $T$, then send a market order." Until the trigger fires, the order is invisible to the exchange's matching engine.

- A **buy stop** above the market is used to enter on breakout or to cover a short.
- A **sell stop** below the market is the classic stop-loss for a long position.

The danger: once the stop is triggered, the resulting market order will fill at whatever liquidity exists. In a fast move (a news gap, a flash crash) your stop-loss at \$98 can easily fill at \$94.

## Stop-limit order

A **stop-limit order** is the same trigger as a stop, but the resulting order is a limit, not a market.

- Pro: you cap the worst price you accept.
- Con: in a fast move the market may blow past your limit and your order never fills — leaving you with the position you tried to exit.

Use stop-limit when you would rather hold the position than be filled at a runaway price. Use plain stop when getting out matters more than the exit price.

## Advanced (intro only): TWAP, VWAP, Iceberg

When you trade institutional size, sending one big order is a great way to move the market against yourself. Three common algorithmic orders solve different parts of this problem:

- **TWAP (Time-Weighted Average Price)** — splits the parent order into small child orders spread evenly over a time window. Your average fill targets the time-weighted average price.
- **VWAP (Volume-Weighted Average Price)** — slices the order in proportion to expected intraday volume (more at the open and close, less at lunch).
- **Iceberg** — shows only a small slice of the order on the book at a time; as each slice fills, the next slice is revealed. Hides true size from other participants.

You will not need these in Track A, but recognize the names when you see them.

## Pitfalls of each order type

<Pitfall title="The four classic order-type mistakes">
1. Market orders on illiquid names — you become the slippage you feared.
2. Limit orders at the wrong side of the spread that never fill, while the market runs away.
3. Stop orders during overnight halts or news gaps — they fill far beyond your trigger.
4. Stop-limit orders in a panic — they don't fill at all, and you keep the loss you tried to exit.
</Pitfall>

<KeyPoint title="Two questions before every order">
Ask yourself: (1) Do I care more about getting in/out, or about the price? (2) How wide is the spread relative to my expected edge? The answers pick the order type for you.
</KeyPoint>

## Decision tree: which order, when?

<Reveal summary="Click to expand the decision tree">

- **Need to trade right now, size is small relative to the book?**
  - Yes → market order.
- **Have a target price and willing to wait?**
  - Yes → limit order at that price.
- **Holding a position and want a defined exit if it goes wrong?**
  - Yes, and getting out matters most → stop order.
  - Yes, and avoiding a bad fill matters most → stop-limit order.
- **Working a large parent order over the day?**
  - Even pacing → TWAP.
  - Following volume profile → VWAP.
  - Need to hide size → iceberg.

</Reveal>
